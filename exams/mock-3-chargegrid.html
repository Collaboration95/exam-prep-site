Architecting + Platform

Question 1 Total: 25 marks

Refer to the case study in Appendix A and answer the following questions:

a.

ChargeGrid's platform value grows with every station and roaming partner it connects.

i.

After a regional power cut, power returned to about 2,000 stations within one minute. Every station reconnected and resent its queued meter values immediately. For the next 25 minutes the station gateway and the session service kept crashing and restarting, and drivers elsewhere could not start sessions. Give the most likely cause and one countermeasure.

2 marksDiagnose an observationArchFailure sources in distributed systemsArchAvailability tactics and stability patternsPlatformCircuit breaker
Model answer and marking points

Cause: a reconnect storm (thundering herd) turning into a restart cascade. Thousands of stations connected and flushed backlogs at the same instant, far above normal load. The gateway exhausted memory and connections and crashed. Its restart dropped every connection, the stations reconnected together again, and the cycle repeated. Overloaded session-service pods failed their health checks and were restarted mid-recovery, which made it worse.

Countermeasure: spread and shed the load. Stations reconnect with exponential backoff plus random jitter, and the platform tells them when to retry. The gateway caps the connection-accept rate and sheds or queues excess load (backpressure), accepting backlog uploads only at a throttled rate. Separate pools (bulkheads) protect start/stop from backlog ingestion. Readiness probes stop overloaded pods taking traffic instead of killing them.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: synchronised reconnects and backlog flush overload the gateway; restarts repeat the surge (cascading failure).
  2. 1 markCountermeasure: jittered backoff, admission or rate control and load shedding, bulkheads, or sensible probes, explained.
ii.

An architect proposes one shared database for SessionManager, StationManager and BillingManager ‘so that billing is always consistent with sessions’. You recommend a database per service, with domain events and a saga for Stop Session → Bill → Capture Payment. Give two reasons.

2 marksDefend a choice against an alternativeCloud nativeData store, CQRS and saga patternsPlatformDistributed transactions and the Saga patternPlatformAPI gateway, orchestration and distributed transactions
Model answer and marking points

1. Isolation and independent scaling: station heartbeats and meter values (thousands of writes per minute) would share one database with billing transactions, so a telemetry surge, like the reconnect storm, would slow or block billing. Schema changes in one service would force coordinated releases of all three. Separate stores let each scale, change and fail on its own.

2. A shared database doesn't make the flow atomic anyway: payment capture happens at an external gateway, which cannot join a local transaction or a two-phase commit. A saga makes the steps explicit: SessionStopped → InvoiceCreated → PaymentCaptured, with compensations (void the invoice, refund, flag for retry) when a later step fails. Consistency is achieved, and failures are handled deliberately instead of assumed away.

Marking points

  1. 1 markIsolation or independent scaling and deployment, using the case's load.
  2. 1 markExternal payment can't be in a local transaction; saga with compensations gives consistency.
b.

With reference to the domain model in Section 7, analyse the following issues. For each, identify the most likely cause, propose a countermeasure by revising the domain model, and suggest its major drawback(s), if any.

i.

Drivers dispute bills: a driver who started a session at 18:00 at $0.45/kWh was billed $0.60/kWh for the whole session because the site host changed the tariff at 18:30. The same happens to idle fees.

5 marksIssue → cause → countermeasure → drawbackPlatformAggregatesPlatformDecoupling services with context mappingsPlatformDDD strategic and tactical design
Model answer and marking points

Cause: ChargingSession and Invoice both hold a live reference to the mutable Tariff entity in TariffManager (‘priced by’, ‘computed with’). The price is looked up when the invoice is computed at stop time, so any change during the session is applied retroactively. The price agreed at start is never recorded. Pricing knowledge leaks across three contexts.

Countermeasure: price is part of the session's own data. Make Tariff versioned: TariffVersion (versionId, pricePerKwh, idleFee, effectiveFrom), immutable once published. At start, ChargingSession captures an immutable PriceSnapshot value object (or the tariffVersionId plus a copy of the prices) inside its own aggregate. Invoice is computed from the session's snapshot and meter values, with no association to Tariff. TariffManager publishes TariffPublished events for display and future sessions.

Drawbacks: duplicated price data in every session. A legitimate mid-session change, such as a regulator-mandated cap, now needs an explicit process. Every client has to handle tariff versioning, and the snapshot schema becomes part of the session contract.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: a live association to a mutable Tariff from Session and Invoice; the price is resolved at stop time.
  2. 1 markNames the modelling principle: the agreed price belongs to the session aggregate (snapshot or value object, not a reference).
  3. 2 marksRevised model: versioned immutable tariffs, a PriceSnapshot or tariffVersionId in the session, Invoice computed from the session, Tariff association removed.
  4. 1 markDrawback: duplication, handling legitimate mid-session changes, contract and versioning effort.
ii.

The Phase 2 smart-charging prototype must keep each site under its grid cap and react to a utility demand-response signal within 60 seconds. In tests it takes 2–4 minutes to compute a site's current load, and the team says the model ‘has nowhere to put’ the charging limits sent to stations.

4 marksIssue → cause → countermeasure → drawbackCloud nativeData store, CQRS and saga patternsPlatformDomain eventsCloud nativeLambda, Kappa, IoT, event-driven, multi-cluster, AI
Model answer and marking points

Cause: the model has no concept of a site's live load. MeterValues exist only inside each ChargingSession aggregate in SessionManager, connector status lives in StationManager, and the grid cap is an attribute of Site. Computing a site's load means querying every active session's latest meter value across contexts and joining with station data on every decision, which is slow and gets worse with scale. There is also no entity for the limit sent to a station.

Countermeasure: add a SmartCharging context with a SiteLoad aggregate (siteId, gridCapKw, currentKw, perConnector loads), kept current from MeterValueReported and ConnectorStatusChanged events: a read model, updated in memory per site. Add ChargingProfile (connectorId, maxKw, validFrom, validTo, reason) as the command the context issues to stations, and DemandResponseEvent from the utility. Decisions then read one aggregate per site.

Drawback: the load view is only as fresh as the meter interval (30 s) plus event lag, which leaves only part of the 60 s budget, so the system must act on estimates. There is extra infrastructure and duplicated state. Profiles need acknowledgement tracking, because a station may be offline.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: load data scattered (meter values per session, status in StationManager); a cross-context join on each decision; no entity for limits.
  2. 2 marksRevised model: a SmartCharging context with a SiteLoad aggregate fed by events, plus ChargingProfile and DemandResponseEvent entities.
  3. 1 markDrawback: staleness relative to the 60 s budget, duplication, profile delivery and acknowledgement complexity.

Model answer and marking points

1. Not Level 2. URIs name actions (startCharging, stop, getStationStatus). GET changes state (stop), POST is used for a read (status), and errors come back as HTTP 200 with an error body, so clients, retries and monitoring can't tell success from failure. Security and design: the partner secret is in the query string, where it is logged. Responses over-share (driver name, card digits). Start blocks for up to 20 s.

2. Asynchronous request-reply. POST /v1/sessions validates the request and returns 202 Accepted at once, with Location: /v1/sessions/{id} and status pending. The platform sends the start command to the station. The partner either polls the session resource (with Retry-After) or registers a callback/webhook that is called when the status becomes charging or failed. The POST carries an Idempotency-Key, so a partner retry after a timeout cannot start two sessions, and the station command is idempotent on the session id.

3. Corrected interfaces:

  1. 1 markVerdict with reasons: action URIs, wrong verbs, 200-for-errors (plus the secret in the URL, oversharing).
  2. 2 marksAsync request-reply: 202 + Location, poll or webhook, idempotency on start and stop.
  3. 3 marksCorrected table: resources, correct verbs and status codes, credentials in headers, minimal data, caching on status.
d.

A team member says that because 3,500 stations are already connected without problems, the platform is ‘proven scalable’, and no further scalability testing is needed before opening the Partner API to 12 roaming partners. Do you agree? Justify your answer.

2 marksAgree or disagree with a teammateArchNon-linearity: Amdahl's law and USLArchValidating the design and documenting performance
Model answer and marking points

Disagree. Working at today's load says nothing about the next load level, because scaling is rarely linear: contention and coherency costs (Amdahl's law, the Universal Scalability Law) can flatten or even reverse throughput as load grows. Roaming also adds a different workload: 12 partners at up to 50 requests/s each is about 600 req/s of start/stop/status traffic, plus their drivers' sessions, with a new failure mode (partner retries). Load-test the new mix at and beyond the expected peak, fit the scalability curve from a few measured points, and set per-partner quotas.

Marking points

  1. 1 markVerdict: current success doesn't extrapolate; scalability is non-linear (USL or Amdahl).
  2. 1 markIdentifies the new workload (≈ 600 req/s partner traffic) and proposes testing or modelling it.
e.

A team member proposes using the CPU utilisation of the session service both as its autoscaling trigger and as the key indicator of drivers' experience when starting a session. Do you agree? Justify your answer and propose alternatives where needed.

4 marksAgree or disagree with a teammateArchProcurement lead time and elasticityArchWhat performance means: the core metricsPlatformMetrics
Model answer and marking points

Partly disagree. CPU is a reasonable resource metric, but the session service mostly waits: on stations (up to 20 s), the payment gateway and the database. It can be saturated (all workers or connections busy, requests queuing) while CPU sits at 30%. Scaling on CPU would then not add capacity when it is needed.

As an autoscaling trigger: use a signal that tracks the work waiting: in-flight requests per pod, request queue length or worker pool utilisation, or consumer lag for event-driven parts. Add scheduled pre-scaling for the evening peak, because scale-out takes time.

As the driver-experience indicator: CPU is not an experience metric at all. Measure start-session latency from tap to charging confirmed (p95 against 5 s), the start success rate, and errors by cause (station timeout, payment declined).

Marking points

  1. 1 markVerdict with reason: an I/O-bound service saturates without high CPU.
  2. 1 markBetter autoscaling signal (concurrency, queue or lag) plus scheduled scaling.
  3. 2 marksExperience metrics: p95 tap-to-charging vs 5 s, success rate, error breakdown, so user-facing vs resource metrics are separated.

Section B 13 marks

Cloud native

Question 2 Total: 13 marks

With reference to the case study in Appendix A, answer the following questions. ChargeGrid is moving from its prototype to a cloud native platform ready for roaming and smart charging (Phase 2).

a.

The prototype is shown below. Stations hold a persistent WebSocket connection to the server.

Transform the prototype into a cloud native reference architecture for Phases 1 and 2. Hint: consider microservices, event/stream-driven services, scheduled tasks and serverless functions.

i.

Draw the reference architecture with its essential components and interactions. Which additional components are essential to expose the platform safely to roaming partners and the utility?

ii.

How will stations, partners and internal services communicate? Label the communication components and justify the patterns.

iii.

Identify one component that must remember state and propose a state-management mechanism.

iv.

Describe the deployment on Kubernetes.

10 marksDesign under constraints, then side effectsCloud nativeLambda, Kappa, IoT, event-driven, multi-cluster, AICloud nativeAPI gateway, versioning and service discoveryCloud nativeServerless architecture patternsCloud nativeState: pets vs cattleCloud nativePods, Services and IngressCloud nativeCloud-native building blocks and ecosystem
Model answer and marking points

i. Components. A station gateway holds the stations' persistent connections (mTLS per station). The API gateway serves the driver app and fleet portal. A separate partner gateway exposes the Partner API with OAuth2 client credentials, per-partner quotas, request validation and API versioning. Services: Session, Station, Tariff, Billing, Driver and, in Phase 2, Smart charging. An event stream carries meter values, status changes and session events. A time-series store holds meter values, and relational stores hold sessions and billing. Functions produce receipts and invoice PDFs; a CronJob runs monthly fleet invoicing. Externally facing essentials: the partner gateway with quotas and a developer portal, webhook delivery with signing and retries for callbacks, an inbound adapter for the utility's demand-response signals, and audit logging of partner calls. The payment gateway uses tokenisation, so card data never enters the platform (keeping it out of PCI scope).

ii. Communication. Stations use bidirectional messages over their persistent WebSocket (station protocol): commands such as start, stop or set-profile carry correlation ids and acknowledgements, and telemetry flows up. The gateway turns telemetry into events on the stream and routes commands to the right connection. Partners use async request-reply (202 + callback). Internally, Session, Billing and Smart charging react to events (choreography), and the stop → bill → pay flow runs as a saga. Synchronous REST only for reads where a user waits.

iii. State. The station gateway is stateful: each station's WebSocket lives on exactly one pod. Keep a connection registry (stationId → pod) in a replicated in-memory store, updated on connect and disconnect, so commands are routed to the right pod (or published on a per-pod topic). Use consistent hashing or session affinity at the load balancer. The station's last known state goes in a durable device shadow owned by the Station service. If a pod dies, its stations reconnect with jittered backoff to other pods and the registry updates.

iv. Kubernetes. A managed cluster across three zones. The station gateway runs as a Deployment behind a layer-4 load balancer with long connection timeouts, graceful draining (preStop hooks, slow rollouts) and a PodDisruptionBudget, scaled on connection count. Other services are Deployments with HPAs (requests per second or consumer lag), with namespaces per domain and network policies isolating the partner-facing namespace. The stream runs as a StatefulSet or managed service, and invoicing as a CronJob. Secrets come from the KMS, delivery from GitOps, with canary releases for the gateway.

Marking points

  1. 3 marksDiagram: station gateway, API and partner gateways, services per context, stream, time-series and relational stores, functions and CronJob, Phase 2 smart charging.
  2. 1 markPartner and utility essentials: partner gateway with OAuth or quotas or versioning, webhooks, demand-response adapter, tokenised payments, audit.
  3. 2 marksStation WebSocket with commands and acks; async request-reply for partners; internal events and saga; justified.
  4. 2 marksGateway connection state with registry or affinity plus device shadow; reconnection handling.
  5. 2 marksk8s plan: long-lived connection handling (draining, PDB), HPA signals, namespaces or policies, StatefulSet or CronJob.
b.

For charging sessions and their billing, would you use a relational database or a distributed NoSQL store? What are the implications for the rest of the platform?

3 marksA or B, with knock-on effectsCloud nativeChoosing a store: KV, column, document, graph, NewSQLPlatformWhy NoSQL and polyglot data modelsPlatformCAP: consistency vs availability
Model answer and marking points

Relational for sessions and billing, plus a separate time-series store for meter values (polyglot persistence). Sessions and invoices are money. They need transactions over a session and its invoice lines, constraints (one active session per connector), and exact, auditable reporting. The volume (25,000 sessions/day) is small for a relational database. The high-rate meter values (every 30 s per active connector) and station telemetry go to a time-series or wide-column store, partitioned by station and day.

Implications: two stores to operate and keep consistent. The session keeps only totals, and invoices are computed from validated meter data. Use an outbox or events to move data between them, and accept that analytics joins happen in a separate reporting store. For partner traffic, scale reads with replicas or a cache, and keep writes on the primary.

Marking points

  1. 1 markChoice justified by transactional money data and constraints (and volume).
  2. 1 markRecognises the different workload (meter values) and gives it a fitting store.
  3. 1 markKnock-on effects: consistency between stores (outbox or events), reporting path, read scaling.

Section C 12 marks

Architecting

Question 3 Total: 12 marks

Refer to the logical architecture in Section 6, the domain model in Section 7 and the packaging model in Section 8, and answer the following questions:

a.

Evaluate the incomplete and potentially erroneous logical packaging cum deployment model for the Start Charging Session use case (Figure 3). Identify two undesirable architectural decisions, propose corrective actions, and justify how each decision leads to your correction.

6 marksCritique and correct an artefactArchLogical architecture: deployment, verify, validate, reviewArchSecure design principles and reference architecturesArchStructural styles and deployment componentsCloud nativeShared responsibility, enterprise concerns and CSA
Model answer and marking points

Any two, each as decision → consequence → correction:

1. Payment Control (PaymentAuthoriser) is deployed on the Station Gateway node in the DMZ, together with PaymentMethod. Payment logic and card-related data would sit on the most exposed node, which takes connections from thousands of field devices. That pulls the DMZ into PCI scope and gives an attacker who breaks the gateway a direct path to payments. Defence in depth and segregation are lost. Correction: the DMZ node runs only connection handling and protocol translation. Payment Control moves to the Billing service in the internal zone and talks to the payment gateway using tokens only. PaymentMethod (token only) is owned by Driver data.

2. Session Control (SessionController) is deployed to the charging station. The station would decide whether a session may start and how it is priced. Firmware is hard to update, may be tampered with in the field, and is offline at times. Correction: authorisation and pricing decisions go server-side in the Session service. The station only executes commands (start, stop, apply a profile) and reports meter values.

3. Tariff is manifested by Session Data Access. That is the same ownership error as the live-tariff bug. Correction: Tariff belongs to Tariff Data Access, and the session stores only its price snapshot.

Marking points

  1. 3 marksFirst decision: problem, consequence (PCI scope, exposure, trust or ownership) and corrected placement or packaging.
  2. 3 marksSecond decision: problem, consequence and correction, justified.
b.

At today's evening peak, start requests arrive at 6 per second. Each start holds a worker in the session service for 4 seconds on average, blocked waiting for the station's confirmation. The service runs 2 pods with 16 workers each. Use Little's Law to compute the worker utilisation now and at twice today's peak (expected after roaming opens), and explain what will happen to drivers. Propose a design that keeps start latency under 5 s (p95) at twice the peak, and identify two side effects to monitor.

6 marksDesign under constraints, then side effectsArchLittle's Law and the General Response Time LawArchMultiple servers, queue layouts and price/performanceArchPerformance tactics and load balancingArchAvailability tactics and stability patterns
Model answer and marking points

Now: Little's Law, N = X × R: 6/s × 4 s = 24 busy workers of 32, so utilisation is 75%. Queueing is already significant: with random arrivals, waits grow steeply above about 70–80%, which explains occasional slow starts.

At 2× peak: 12/s × 4 s = 48 workers needed but only 32 exist, so U > 100%. The queue grows without limit during the peak, waits climb into tens of seconds, starts time out, drivers retry, and the retries add more load. The service collapses until the peak passes.

Design:

  1. Stop holding workers while waiting: make the start flow asynchronous. Accept the request, send the command, and return 202 with the session in pending. The station's confirmation arrives as an event and is pushed to the app. Service time per request drops from 4 s to a few milliseconds of CPU, so a handful of workers handles 12/s.
  2. Bound the wait itself: the station confirmation time (4 s average) still counts toward the driver's 5 s. Set command timeouts, and alert on stations that are slow to confirm.
  3. If the blocking style must stay for now: size by Little's Law with headroom, ⌈48 / 0.7⌉ ≈ 69 workers (about 5 pods of 16). Add a separate pool (bulkhead) for start/stop, apart from status reads, and scale on in-flight requests.

Side effects to monitor: (1) duplicate starts: retries plus async commands can start twice, so the session id must be idempotent at the station; count duplicate commands. (2) Pending sessions that never resolve, when a station never confirms: track pending age and add a timeout sweeper with compensation. (3) Push delivery failures to the app: monitor delivery rate and provide a polling fallback. (4) Event lag on the confirmation path against the 5 s budget.

Marking points

  1. 2 marksCorrect Little's Law: 24 of 32 = 75% now; 48 needed > 32 at 2× peak, so unbounded queueing, with consequences explained.
  2. 2 marksDesign: non-blocking async start (202 + push), or correctly sized pools, bulkheads and scaling, with the station's own latency considered.
  3. 2 marksTwo side effects with monitoring (duplicate starts or idempotency, stuck pending, push failures, lag).
