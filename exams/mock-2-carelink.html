Architecting + Platform

Question 1 Total: 25 marks

Refer to the case study in Appendix A and answer the following questions:

a.

CareLink's value to the hospital cluster depends on nurses seeing a patient's deterioration promptly.

i.

In user-acceptance testing, the nurses' vitals dashboard showed gaps of 20–60 seconds for some patients, although the wearables had recorded every reading. The phone app uploads readings in HTTP batches every 30 seconds and buffers them while offline (Section 4). The gateway logs show bursts of HTTP 429 responses for the same phones at the start of each gap. Give the most likely cause and one countermeasure.

2 marksDiagnose an observationPlatformRate limitingPlatformErrors, logging, retries, pagination and monitoringArchFailure sources in distributed systems
Model answer and marking points

Cause: the API gateway's per-client rate limit (a token bucket sized for one batch every 30 s) is rejecting phones that flush a backlog. After a phone has been offline, it sends many buffered batches at once, exceeds the bucket and gets 429. The app gives up or drops those batches after a few retries, so the readings never arrive and the dashboard shows gaps.

Countermeasure: treat 429 as ‘slow down’, not ‘discard’. The app keeps the readings, retries with exponential backoff and jitter, honours Retry-After, and merges the backlog into larger compressed batches. On the platform side, set the ingestion limits per device to allow a catch-up burst (a bigger bucket, or a separate catch-up endpoint), or move ingestion to a streaming connection with acknowledgements.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: the rate limiter rejects catch-up bursts from buffered phones, and the client drops rejected data.
  2. 1 markCountermeasure: client backoff with jitter and retain-until-acknowledged, and/or rate-limit policy tuned for catch-up bursts or streaming ingestion with acks.
ii.

A developer proposes storing the vital-sign readings in the same relational database as patient records, ‘to keep everything in one place’. You recommend a time-series or wide-column store partitioned by patient and day instead. Give two reasons.

2 marksDefend a choice against an alternativePlatformColumn family (wide column) storesCloud nativeChoosing a store: KV, column, document, graph, NewSQLPlatformWhat we need from a datastore and where RDBMS stands
Model answer and marking points

1. Write pattern and volume: 12,000 patients × one reading every 5 s is about 2,400 appends per second, all day, growing to 4,800. A wide-column or time-series store is built for high-rate, append-only writes, spread across nodes by partition key (patient, day). A single relational primary would need sharding work and would compete with transactional patient updates.

2. Query pattern and retention: almost every read is ‘patient P between t1 and t2’. With the readings clustered by patient and time inside each partition, that is a sequential slice, fast enough for 2-year queries in under 3 s. Time-based retention (TTL), downsampling and tiering also come built in. The cost: no joins with patient data and weaker ad hoc queries, so reporting needs a separate path.

Marking points

  1. 1 markWrite throughput and append-only scaling (with the case's numbers).
  2. 1 markTime-range reads by patient, retention, TTL and downsampling, or isolation from the transactional DB.
b.

With reference to the domain model in Section 7, analyse the following issues. For each, identify the most likely cause, propose a countermeasure by revising the domain model, and suggest its major drawback(s), if any.

i.

Clinicians want to add blood glucose and respiratory rate. The team estimates that each new vital sign needs code changes and redeployment in four services, and that adding an alert threshold for blood pressure needs another release of AlertManager.

5 marksIssue → cause → countermeasure → drawbackArchDefer binding: customisation and workflow enginesArchCode-level maintainability tacticsPlatformDesigning frameworks well
Model answer and marking points

Cause: vital types are hard-coded in the model. Each vital is a subclass (HeartRateReading, SpO2Reading, BloodPressureReading) with its own association to Patient, and each alert rule is a type-specific subclass (HeartRateRule, SpO2Rule) bound to one reading class. A new vital means a new class, a new association and new rule types across Monitoring, Alert, NurseConsole and the dashboards. Binding happens at compile time.

Countermeasure: generalise and defer the binding to run time. Replace the subclasses with one Observation (patientRef, typeCode, value, unit, takenAt) and an ObservationType reference entity (code, unit, valid range, display name), managed as data. Replace the rule subclasses with a single AlertRule (typeCode, operator, threshold, duration, severity) evaluated generically. Patient has one association to Observation. Adding glucose is then a new ObservationType row and new AlertRule rows.

Drawbacks: weaker compile-time typing and validation: multi-value vitals such as blood pressure need components or a structured value, and a wrong unit is caught later. Queries and indexes become more generic. Clinical governance of the type and rule configuration is needed, since a bad rule is now a data change, not a reviewed release.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: type-specific subclasses and rule subclasses hard-wired across services (compile-time binding).
  2. 1 markExplains the ripple: new class, associations and rules in several services, so coordinated releases.
  3. 2 marksRevised model: a generic Observation plus ObservationType reference data and a parameterised AlertRule, configured at run time.
  4. 1 markDrawback: weaker typing, complex multi-value vitals, governance of configuration.
ii.

Nurses sometimes see the same alert twice on their console, and after a failover of the console service an alert that had already been acknowledged re-appeared as new.

4 marksIssue → cause → countermeasure → drawbackPlatformAggregatesPlatformDomain eventsPlatformIntegration styles between contexts
Model answer and marking points

Cause: the alert exists twice. AlertManager owns Alert, and NurseConsole keeps a ConsoleAlert copy with its own number and status, linked by a ‘copies’ association. Acknowledgement updates only the copy. Copies are created per delivery, so a redelivered message creates a second ConsoleAlert. After failover the console rebuilds from AlertManager, where the alert was never acknowledged.

Countermeasure: one Alert aggregate in AlertManager with a business identity (patientId + ruleId + breach window) and a lifecycle Raised → Acknowledged → Resolved, with acknowledgedBy and acknowledgedAt. Acknowledge is a command to AlertManager. NurseConsole keeps only a read model keyed by alertId, updated from AlertRaised and AlertAcknowledged events with idempotent upserts, so a duplicate event changes nothing.

Drawback: acknowledging now depends on AlertManager being available (queue the command, or show it as pending). The console view lags by the event delay. Idempotency keys and the lifecycle add design and test effort.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: duplicated alert state (ConsoleAlert copy) with no shared identity; acknowledgement stored only in the copy.
  2. 2 marksRevised model: a single Alert aggregate with business key and lifecycle; the console as an idempotent read model fed by events; acknowledge as a command.
  3. 1 markDrawback: dependency on AlertManager for acknowledge, event lag, complexity.

  2. Propose how the history API should meet ‘< 3 s for 90%’ for any window up to two years.

6 marksCritique and correct an artefactPlatformResource naming, HTTP methods and idempotencyPlatformErrors, logging, retries, pagination and monitoringPlatformPerformance, testing and API architecture maturityCloud nativeData security and encryption
Model answer and marking points

1. Problems. Action-named URIs (getVitals, delete, ack) rather than resources. GET changes state (ack), which is unsafe: link prefetchers and retries could acknowledge alerts. POST is used to delete. String responses carry no status codes. Security: the NRIC in the query string is logged by proxies and gateways and cached by browsers. The response returns name and address that a vitals chart doesn't need (data minimisation). The acting nurse's identity is a free-text input rather than taken from the authenticated token, so the audit can be spoofed. Clinical data should be corrected by marking a reading as erroneous, with an audit trail, not deleted.

2. History performance: never return 12 million rows. Make the resolution depend on the window: raw readings for short windows, and pre-computed 1-minute, 1-hour or 1-day aggregates for longer ones (precomputation). Use cursor pagination with a page-size cap, and compression. Store the data partitioned by patient and day so a window is a sequential read. Responses for closed past windows never change, so serve them with long cache lifetimes and ETags.

3. Corrected interfaces:

  1. 2 marksREST/RMM problems: action URIs, unsafe GET, POST for delete, no status codes. Security: NRIC in URL, oversharing, identity not from the token, delete vs audit.
  2. 2 marksPerformance plan: resolution-dependent aggregates, pagination, partitioned storage, caching or ETags for immutable windows.
  3. 2 marksCorrected table with resource URIs, verbs, opaque ids, token-derived identity and proper responses.
d.

A team member argues that because alerts must reach the nurse within 10 seconds, all services on the alert path should call each other synchronously, ‘so nothing gets stuck in a queue’. Do you agree? Justify your answer.

2 marksAgree or disagree with a teammateCloud nativeInter-process communicationPlatformIntegration styles between contextsArchAvailability tactics and stability patterns
Model answer and marking points

Disagree. A synchronous chain from ingestion to rule evaluation to alert to console couples the services' availability: the path is only as available as the product of its parts, and one slow service blocks the others. A short outage then loses alerts instead of delaying them. Asynchronous, durable messaging is what keeps alerts safe: an event stream or queue persists each breach until it is processed, and millisecond-level latencies easily fit the 10-second budget. Keep synchronous calls only where a user waits for an answer, and monitor end-to-end latency and consumer lag against the 10 s / 99% target.

Marking points

  1. 1 markVerdict: synchronous chains multiply failure and latency; durable async does not ‘lose’ messages.
  2. 1 markTies to the NFR: async latency fits 10 s; measure end-to-end latency and lag.
e.

A team member proposes publishing the number of alerts raised per day as the key metric showing that the monitoring service is reliable. Do you agree? If not, what would show reliability?

4 marksAgree or disagree with a teammatePlatformMetricsArchAvailability, reliability and fault toleranceArchAnalyzability: detecting and diagnosing problems
Model answer and marking points

Disagree. The daily alert count depends mostly on how sick the patients are and how the thresholds are set. It rises during a flu wave and falls when rules are relaxed. A service that silently misses half the breaches could show a ‘healthy’ count.

Reliability is whether the service keeps doing the right thing over time. Measure: missed-alert rate, from synthetic or injected breaches and audits of breaches without alerts; end-to-end alert latency (p99 against 10 s); duplicate and false-alert rates; ingestion completeness (readings received vs expected per device); and service availability, MTBF and MTTR of the alert path. Add consumer lag and dead-letter counts as leading indicators.

Keep the alert count as an operational workload measure for staffing, not as evidence of reliability.

Marking points

  1. 1 markVerdict with reason: the count reflects patient condition or configuration, not correct operation.
  2. 1 markShows the failure mode it hides (missed alerts).
  3. 2 marksProper measures: missed-alert rate or synthetic probes, p99 latency vs 10 s, duplicates and false alerts, completeness, availability, MTTR.

Section B 13 marks

Cloud native

Question 2 Total: 13 marks

With reference to the case study in Appendix A, answer the following questions. The hospital's EMR and pharmacy systems will stay on premises; the rest of CareLink is to be cloud native, and Phase 2 adds GenAI-drafted consultation notes.

a.

The pilot architecture is shown below.

Transform it into a hybrid cloud native reference architecture for Phases 1 and 2. Hint: consider microservices, stream processing, serverless functions, scheduled jobs and integration with the on-premises systems.

i.

Draw the reference architecture with its essential components and interactions. Which components are essential because the EMR and pharmacy systems remain on premises?

ii.

How will readings flow from wearables to the nurse's console, and how will the internal services communicate? Label this in your diagram and justify the patterns.

iii.

Identify one service that must remember state and propose a suitable state-management mechanism.

iv.

Describe the Kubernetes deployment plan for the cloud part.

10 marksDesign under constraints, then side effectsCloud nativeHybrid, multi-cloud and reference architecturesCloud nativeMigration options II: Rearchitect to EncapsulateCloud nativeLLM vs RAG vs Agents, and what GenAI isCloud nativeInter-process communicationCloud nativeCloud native design on KubernetesCloud nativeShared responsibility, enterprise concerns and CSA
Model answer and marking points

i. Components. Patient apps and wearables reach a streaming ingestion endpoint. Clinician web apps go through CDN + WAF and the API gateway, which uses the hospital identity provider via OIDC. Services: Patient, Consult (with a managed video service), Prescription, Ingestion, Alert engine, Nurse console BFF and Notification. An event stream carries vitals and alerts. A time-series store holds the vitals; Patient and Alert data sit in relational stores. Functions handle push and SMS; a scheduled job reconciles with the EMR nightly. Phase 2 adds a note-summariser service using RAG over clinical guidelines in a vector store, behind an AI gateway that redacts identifiers before calling an in-region model endpoint. On-premises essentials: private connectivity (VPN or dedicated link) with redundant paths; an EMR facade that encapsulates the legacy system behind a stable API, with change data capture to keep cloud copies current; identity federation with the hospital directory; and data-residency controls keeping everything in-country, with key management the hospital controls.

ii. Flow. Wearable → phone over BLE. The phone streams batches over MQTT or HTTPS, acknowledged per batch so nothing is dropped. Ingestion publishes VitalsReceived to the stream, partitioned by patient. The Alert engine consumes it, evaluates the rules and publishes AlertRaised. The Nurse console BFF consumes that and pushes to the nurse's screen over WebSocket. Notification also consumes it to page the on-call nurse. Internally: events and choreography for the data flow; synchronous REST/gRPC only for user queries (history, patient details); a queue with retries for paging commands.

iii. State. The Alert engine must remember recent readings per patient to evaluate rules like ‘SpO2 < 92% for 60 s’. Use stateful stream processing: keyed state per patient in an embedded state store, checkpointed to durable storage, and partitions reassigned on failure so another instance restores the state and resumes from the last checkpoint. The pods themselves stay replaceable.

iv. Kubernetes. A managed cluster in the in-country region across three zones, with namespaces for ingestion, clinical, AI and platform. Deployments with at least 3 replicas and an HPA on consumer lag (ingestion, alert engine) or requests per second (APIs). The alert engine runs as a StatefulSet if it keeps local state stores. The GPU node pool is separate, and only if the model is self-hosted. Network policies isolate the clinical namespaces, secrets come from the KMS, and PodDisruptionBudgets apply. GitOps handles delivery, with canary releases for the alert engine guarded by synthetic-breach tests.

Marking points

  1. 3 marksDiagram: ingestion, stream, alert engine, BFF with push, services per context, stores, functions or jobs, Phase 2 RAG and AI gateway.
  2. 1 markHybrid essentials: private redundant connectivity, EMR facade (encapsulate) or CDC, identity federation, data residency or keys.
  3. 2 marksFlow: acknowledged streaming ingestion → events → push to console; sync only for queries; justified.
  4. 2 marksStateful component with a fitting mechanism (keyed, checkpointed stream state, or a session store), with how it recovers.
  5. 2 marksCoherent k8s plan: region and zones, namespaces, HPA on lag, StatefulSet where needed, isolation, rollout.
b.

For the Phase 2 Draft Consultation Note use case, would you call an externally hosted large language model API, or self-host an open model inside your Kubernetes cluster? What are the implications for the rest of the platform?

3 marksA or B, with knock-on effectsCloud nativeModel families, value and the GenAI stackCloud nativeLLM vs RAG vs Agents, and what GenAI isCloud nativeData security and encryption
Model answer and marking points

It depends on residency; with in-country guarantees, use a hosted endpoint. Transcripts are health data that must stay in-country (Section 5). Pick a provider's model endpoint in the in-country region, with contractual no-retention and private connectivity. That avoids running GPUs for a modest load (150 doctors) and gives better models. If no provider can guarantee in-country processing, self-host an open model, accepting the GPU cost, the operational effort and probably lower quality.

Implications: an AI gateway that redacts identifiers, enforces quotas and the cost cap, and logs prompts and outputs for audit. A RAG pipeline (guidelines in an in-region vector store) so drafts are grounded. Prompt and model versioning with an evaluation set before upgrades. Graceful degradation: the note is a draft, so if the model is slow or unavailable the doctor writes it manually and nothing blocks the consultation. A human review step before the note reaches the EMR.

Marking points

  1. 1 markDecision driven by data residency or PDPA and load or cost, with the condition that would flip it.
  2. 1 markTrade-off: control and residency vs cost, GPU operations and model quality.
  3. 1 markKnock-on effects: AI gateway with redaction and audit, RAG store, evaluation and versioning, fallback, human review.

Section C 12 marks

Architecting

Question 3 Total: 12 marks

Refer to the logical architecture in Section 6, the domain model in Section 7 and the packaging model in Section 8, and answer the following questions:

a.

Evaluate the incomplete and potentially erroneous logical packaging cum deployment model for the Raise Alert use case (Figure 3). Identify two undesirable architectural decisions, propose corrective actions, and justify how each decision leads to your correction.

6 marksCritique and correct an artefactArchLogical architecture: deployment, verify, validate, reviewArchLogical architecture: overview and functional elementsArchAccess control and network segregationCloud nativeData store, CQRS and saga patterns
Model answer and marking points

Any two, each as decision → consequence → correction:

1. Alert Evaluation Control (RuleEvaluator) runs only on the patient's phone. A phone with a flat battery, killed app or no signal raises no alert at all. Rule changes need app releases, and an outdated phone evaluates outdated thresholds. Correction: evaluate rules server-side in the Alert service, on the ingested stream. The phone may show local warnings to the patient, but it is not the authority.

2. Alert is manifested by both Alert Manager Data Access and Nurse Console Data Access. Two owners of the same entity is the duplicated-state problem behind double alerts and lost acknowledgements. Correction: Alert belongs only to Alert Manager Data Access. The nurse console holds a read model fed by events.

3. Nurse Console Data Access is deployed to the ward workstation. Patient alerts would be stored on a shared ward PC: data at rest outside the controlled zone, no central audit, and alerts lost if the PC fails. Correction: keep data access in the internal zone. The workstation runs only the console presentation, which receives pushes from the BFF.

4. AlertBoard (Nurse Console Control) uses VitalReading from Monitoring's data directly. That couples it to Monitoring's schema and bypasses its access rules. Correction: go through Monitoring's API, or a read model of recent vitals.

Marking points

  1. 3 marksFirst decision: problem, consequence (missed alerts, ownership, data exposure) and corrected placement or packaging.
  2. 3 marksSecond decision: problem, consequence and correction, justified by the case NFRs or security.
b.

Each reading record (heart rate, SpO2, temperature and motion, with keys and timestamp) takes 64 bytes. Use the figures in Section 4: readings every 5 s from 12,000 concurrent patients today, growing linearly to 24,000 by the end of year 2; two years online; replication factor 3; index overhead 30%. Estimate the online storage needed at the end of year 2. Propose a storage plan that keeps queries under 3 s and controls cost, and identify two side effects to monitor.

6 marksDesign under constraints, then side effectsArchPlanning disk capacityArchTrend analysis and forecasting methodsArchCaching and precomputationCloud nativeCloud persistence options: file storage types
Model answer and marking points

Estimate: per patient 86,400 / 5 = 17,280 records/day × 64 B ≈ 1.1 MB/day. Today: 12,000 × 1.1 MB ≈ 13.3 GB/day raw. Linear growth to 24,000 gives an average of 18,000 patients over two years: 13.3 × 1.5 ≈ 19.9 GB/day × 730 days ≈ 14.5 TB raw. × 1.3 for indexes ≈ 18.9 TB. × 3 replicas ≈ 57 TB. Add 20–30% headroom for compaction and growth: about 70–75 TB if everything stays raw and online.

Plan:

  1. Hot tier: the last 30 days raw, partitioned by patient and day, on fast storage: about 0.6 TB raw at 24,000 patients ≈ 2.3 TB with indexes and replicas.
  2. Downsample older data: keep 1-minute aggregates (min, max, mean per vital, about 96 B per minute vs 12 × 64 B raw), roughly 8× smaller, for months 2–24. Keep raw data around flagged events (± 30 min around every alert) for clinical review.
  3. Archive raw to compressed object storage (about 5×) with lifecycle rules, for audit and re-processing, outside the 3 s query path.
  4. Query routing: the API picks the resolution from the window size (see Q1c), so long windows read aggregates only. Pre-compute the rollups with a scheduled or streaming job.

Side effects to monitor: (1) loss of short events in downsampled data, so audit that alert windows are kept raw; (2) lag or failures in the rollup jobs, leaving stale or missing aggregates, so track job lag and completeness; (3) slower reads for archived raw data, so set clinician expectations and measure p90 per tier; (4) retention and erasure obligations across tiers and backups, so verify deletion is complete.

Marking points

  1. 2 marksCorrect arithmetic chain: records/day, bytes, growth average, indexes, replicas, headroom (≈ 57 TB before headroom).
  2. 2 marksPlan: hot, downsampled and archive tiers, partitioning, precomputed rollups, query routing by resolution.
  3. 2 marksTwo relevant side effects with what to monitor.
