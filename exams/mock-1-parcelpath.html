Architecting + Platform

Question 1 Total: 25 marks

Refer to the case study in Appendix A and answer the following questions:

a.

The success of ParcelPath as a platform depends on couriers, lockers and merchants interacting reliably at the morning peak.

i.

During load testing of the courier location ping API (each active courier sends one ping every 10 seconds, see Section 4), the 90th-percentile response time was 120 ms with 2,000 simulated couriers. With 5,000 simulated couriers it rose to 9 seconds, although CPU utilisation on the API servers stayed at about 55%.

Give the most likely reason for this observation and one way to confirm it.

2 marksDiagnose an observationArchQueueing theory notation and the operational lawsArchQueues and the M/M/1 response time curveArchNon-linearity: Amdahl's law and USL
Model answer and marking points

Likely reason: the bottleneck is not the API servers but a resource behind them, probably the single relational database. Every ping is a write (an insert or update) into the Courier aggregate's LocationPing table (Figure 2). 5,000 couriers / 10 s = 500 writes/s against 200/s at 2,000 couriers. If each write holds the database or a connection for about 1.9 ms, utilisation goes from about 0.4 to about 0.95. With random arrivals, R = S / (1 − U) grows sharply as U approaches 1, so requests queue for connections and locks while the API servers idle waiting. That explains the jump from 120 ms to 9 s.

Confirm it with the utilisation law, U = X × S, per resource. Measure throughput and busy time (or connection-pool wait, lock waits and disk queue) on the database during the 5,000-courier run. The resource with the highest utilisation is the bottleneck. Its demand per ping should match the observed saturation point.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: a saturated downstream resource (database write path, connection pool or lock contention) where queueing delay explodes near 100% utilisation. CPU on the API tier is not the bottleneck.
  2. 1 markConfirmation: measure per-resource utilisation and queueing (U = X × S, pool wait, lock waits) under load to locate the bottleneck.

Trap: ‘Add more API servers.’ The 55% CPU says the API servers are waiting, not working.

ii.

A developer proposes that each locker controller calls the platform synchronously (REST over 4G) to validate every pickup code entered on the keypad. You recommend instead that lockers validate codes locally, using signed, time-limited codes synchronised to the locker in advance, and report collections to the platform asynchronously. Give two reasons for your recommendation.

2 marksDefend a choice against an alternativeArchAvailability tactics and stability patternsCloud nativeAppendix: local-first, MACH, platform engineering, selectionArchTechnical controls: cryptography primitives
Model answer and marking points

1. Collection must survive a network outage of up to 4 hours (Section 4). With synchronous validation, every collection depends in series on the 4G link, the gateway and the Locker service, so any one failing stops collections. Local validation takes the network off the critical path, and store-and-forward reporting delivers the collection events once the link returns.

2. Latency and load at peak: the compartment must open within 2 s for 95% of attempts. A 4G round trip to the cloud adds variable latency and puts load on the platform at the busiest hour. Validating a signed code locally (a signature or HMAC check against keys provisioned to the locker) takes milliseconds and cannot be forged without the key. The cost: revoking a code is only effective after the next sync, so codes must be short-lived and single-use.

Marking points

  1. 1 markAvailability: removes the network and cloud from the collection path, meeting the 4-hour offline requirement with store-and-forward.
  2. 1 markPerformance or security: meets 2 s p95 locally; signed codes resist forgery; acknowledges the revocation lag.
b.

With reference to the domain model in Section 7 of the case study, analyse the following issues. For each issue, identify the most likely cause, propose an effective countermeasure (by revising the domain model) and suggest the major drawback(s) of the countermeasure, if any.

i.

Between 10:00 and 12:00, up to 15 couriers deposit parcels at the same busy locker station. Couriers frequently receive the error ‘compartment status changed, please retry’, and a deposit can take 20 seconds or more.

5 marksIssue → cause → countermeasure → drawbackPlatformAggregatesPlatformCar rental example and workshopPlatformBounded contexts and ubiquitous language in depth
Model answer and marking points

Cause: LockerStation is modelled as one aggregate composed of all 60 Compartments, so every deposit at a station updates the same aggregate and conflicts with every other courier's deposit (optimistic-lock failures, retries). Worse, Stop in DeliveryManager is associated directly with Compartment and sets its status, so two bounded contexts write the same data with no single owner of the compartment-allocation rule.

Countermeasure: make Compartment (or a CompartmentReservation) its own small aggregate owned only by LockerManager. LockerStation keeps just station data and references its compartments by id. Replace the Stop–Compartment association with a compartmentId reference. DeliveryManager requests a reservation (a command to LockerManager), and LockerManager allocates a free compartment of the right size and publishes CompartmentReserved and later ParcelDeposited, which DeliveryManager consumes to update the Stop. Concurrent deposits now touch different aggregates.

Drawbacks: station-wide rules, such as ‘never more than N large compartments reserved’, become eventually consistent or need a separate counter. Reservations need expiry and compensation if the courier never arrives. There are more messages and states to test.

Marking points

  1. 1 markIdentifies the oversized LockerStation aggregate (60 compartments as one consistency boundary) causing contention.
  2. 1 markIdentifies the cross-context write: Stop associated with Compartment, so DeliveryManager mutates LockerManager's data.
  3. 2 marksRevised model: Compartment or Reservation as its own aggregate in LockerManager, id references, a reservation command and domain events back to DeliveryManager.
  4. 1 markDrawback: eventual consistency of station-level invariants, reservation expiry and compensation, more messaging.
ii.

At peak, recipients complain that the tracking page takes about 8 seconds to load and that the courier's position shown is often several minutes old, although couriers' phones send pings every 10 seconds.

4 marksIssue → cause → countermeasure → drawbackCloud nativeData store, CQRS and saga patternsCloud nativeContent aggregation patternsPlatformKey-value stores
Model answer and marking points

Cause: TrackingView has no data of its own. It is assembled at request time by following associations into Shipment (ShipmentManager), Stop (DeliveryManager) and LocationPing, which is held inside the Courier aggregate. Each view therefore needs synchronous calls across three services and a scan of a fast-growing ping history. The location writes (500/s) contend with these reads on the same store, so pages are slow, and readers fall back to stale cached positions.

Countermeasure: separate the write and read models. LocationPing leaves the Courier aggregate and becomes a stream of CourierLocated events. TrackingManager owns a TrackingSnapshot read model, one per active shipment, holding status, ETA and latest position, built from ShipmentCreated, StopCompleted and CourierLocated events. Keep it in a key-value store or cache keyed by shipmentNo, so the page is a single key lookup. History, if needed, goes to a time-series store.

Drawback: the snapshot duplicates data and is eventually consistent, a few seconds behind. The projection must handle out-of-order and duplicate events, and the extra store and stream must be run.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: a request-time composition across three contexts plus ping history inside the Courier aggregate (write and read contention, growing data).
  2. 2 marksRevised model: a TrackingSnapshot read model owned by TrackingManager, fed by events; pings out of the Courier aggregate; key lookup by shipment.
  3. 1 markDrawback: duplication, eventual consistency, projection complexity.

  1. Which level of the Richardson Maturity Model does this design reach? Give the reasons.
  2. Propose a mechanism that stops merchant retries from creating duplicate shipments, and explain how it works.

6 marksCritique and correct an artefactPlatformResource naming, HTTP methods and idempotencyPlatformRichardson Maturity ModelPlatformMeaningful responses and status codesPlatformAPI versioning and security basics
Model answer and marking points

1. Level 0 to 1 at best. The URIs name actions (createShipment, cancel) rather than resources, and the HTTP verbs are misused: GET changes state (cancel), POST is used for a read (tracking), and every outcome returns 200 with the error in the body. Level 2 needs resources, correct verbs and meaningful status codes. The API key in the query string also ends up in logs and caches, and /v2.3 exposes a minor version.

2. Idempotency key. The merchant sends an Idempotency-Key header (a UUID per business request, or its own order reference) with the create. ShipmentManager stores the key with the result, atomically with the shipment insert. A retry with the same key and body returns the original 201 response instead of creating a second shipment; the same key with a different body returns 422. Keys are kept for at least the retry window (for example 24 h). The client must reuse the key on retries, not generate a new one.

  2. 2 marksIdempotency-key mechanism explained: stored with the result atomically, replay returns the original response, key scope and expiry.
  3. 3 marksCorrected table: resource URIs, correct verbs, credentials in headers, data minimisation, status codes, major-only versioning.
d.

A team member argues that because lockers must keep working offline, availability is the only non-functional requirement that matters for the locker subsystem. To make offline collection possible, he proposes caching the plain-text pickup codes on each locker controller. Do you agree? Justify your answer.

2 marksAgree or disagree with a teammateArchQuality attributes and the utility treeArchSecure design principles and reference architecturesArchTechnical controls: cryptography primitives
Model answer and marking points

Disagree. Availability is critical, but the lockers hold other people's parcels in public places, so confidentiality and integrity of the codes are just as significant. A stolen or tampered controller with plain-text codes would open every compartment. The two qualities need not conflict: store only hashes or keyed MACs of codes, or verify signed, expiring codes without storing them, with keys in the controller's secure element. Offline collection then works without exposing usable codes. Rank both high in the utility tree.

Marking points

  1. 1 markVerdict: security (confidentiality and integrity of codes) is also architecturally significant, with the threat from physical locker compromise.
  2. 1 markShows both can be met: hashed or signed codes, secure key storage, so availability is not traded for security.
e.

A team member proposes reporting the average delivery time per day (from shipment creation to collection) as the main availability metric of the platform. Do you agree? If not, what would you measure instead?

4 marksAgree or disagree with a teammatePlatformMetricsArchAvailability, reliability and fault toleranceArchAvailability calculations: nines, series and parallel
Model answer and marking points

Disagree. Average delivery time is a business (service-level) outcome driven by recipients, traffic and couriers. A platform could be down for an hour and the daily average would barely move, while a perfectly available platform can still have slow deliveries. An average also hides the tail and the timing of outages.

Availability is the proportion of time, or of requests, in which the platform does its job. Measure: uptime of the locker-operation path against 99.95% (allowed downtime about 22 minutes a month); yield, the share of open-compartment and API requests that succeed; harvest, the completeness of answers when degraded, such as tracking without live position; MTBF and MTTR per component; and the error-budget burn. Report these per locker station and per critical use case, not just overall.

Keep average delivery time as a business KPI next to them.

Marking points

  1. 1 markVerdict: delivery time is a business outcome, not availability; give a counter-example.
  2. 1 markExplains why an average hides outages and tails.
  3. 2 marksProper availability measures: uptime vs target, request success (yield), harvest, MTTR or MTBF, per critical path.

Section B 13 marks

Cloud native

Question 2 Total: 13 marks

With reference to the case study in Appendix A, answer the following questions. The cloud native team is preparing Phase 2 (dynamic re-routing and ETA prediction) and must first fix the Phase 1 prototype.

a.

The prototype built for the pilot is shown below. Lockers poll the server every 5 seconds for commands.

Transform the prototype into a cloud native reference architecture that also covers the locker sites and the hub sorting systems that stay on premises (a hybrid deployment). Hint: consider microservices, event/stream-driven services, scheduled jobs, serverless functions and edge components.

i.

Draw the reference architecture with its essential components and interactions. Which additional components are essential because part of the solution runs at the edge and on premises?

ii.

How will lockers communicate with the platform, and how will the internal services communicate? Label these in your diagram and justify the patterns.

iii.

Identify one component that must remember state and propose a suitable state-management mechanism.

iv.

Describe the deployment of the cloud part on Kubernetes, either as a k8s deployment diagram or as a paragraph.

10 marksDesign under constraints, then side effectsCloud nativeHybrid, multi-cloud and reference architecturesCloud nativeLambda, Kappa, IoT, event-driven, multi-cluster, AICloud nativeInter-process communicationCloud nativeDesign principles and when to use serverlessCloud nativeDesign patterns, state and traffic flowCloud nativeState: pets vs cattle
Model answer and marking points

i. Components. Couriers, recipients and merchants come in through CDN + WAF and the API gateway (merchant credentials, rate limits). Lockers connect to a separate device gateway (an MQTT-style broker) with per-device certificates. Microservices per context: Shipment, Delivery, Locker, Tracking and Billing. An event stream carries scans, location pings and locker events. The route planner is a scheduled batch job (05:00 daily). Functions handle bursty, short work: SMS with pickup codes, shipping-label PDFs. Each service owns its database. Tracking is a read model in a cache, and an object store keeps labels and proof-of-delivery photos. Edge and on-premises essentials: a local agent on each locker with a code cache and store-and-forward queue; a device registry with device shadows; certificate-based device identity and over-the-air update management; a VPN or private link to the hub sorters; and integration adapters for the on-premises systems.

ii. Communication. Lockers use publish/subscribe over the broker. Telemetry and collection events flow up; commands such as ‘open compartment 12’ flow down with correlation ids and acknowledgements. The connection is persistent, so no 5-second polling, and messages are buffered when offline. Internally, synchronous REST/gRPC only where a user waits (Delivery asks Locker to reserve a compartment). Everything else is asynchronous events on the stream (ParcelScanned, CourierLocated, ParcelDeposited), consumed independently by Tracking, Billing and later the Phase 2 re-router. Commands to functions go over a queue with retry and a dead-letter queue.

iii. State. The Locker service must know each locker's last reported state (compartments, firmware, connectivity) even while the locker is offline. Keep a device shadow per locker (desired vs reported state) in a durable store owned by the Locker service: commands update ‘desired’, locker reports update ‘reported’, and the difference is applied on reconnect. The service pods stay stateless. (Alternative: the route planner's job state, with checkpoints in the object store so a failed run restarts from the last zone.)

iv. Kubernetes. A managed cluster across three zones, with namespaces per domain (delivery, lockers, tracking, billing, platform). Services run as Deployments (at least 3 replicas, HPA on requests per second or consumer lag) behind an Ingress fed by the API gateway. The broker and the stream run as StatefulSets with persistent volumes, or as managed services. The route planner is a CronJob with resource requests sized for its 40-minute CPU run and its own node pool. Config and secrets come from ConfigMaps and Secrets, network policies apply between namespaces, and GitOps handles delivery with canary releases. Scheduled scale-up runs before the 10:00 peak.

Marking points

  1. 3 marksDiagram with services per context, gateways, event stream, scheduled job, functions and data stores, with sensible interactions.
  2. 1 markEdge and on-premises essentials: local agent and buffering, device registry or shadow, device identity and OTA, VPN/private link, adapters.
  3. 2 marksLocker pub/sub with commands and acks instead of polling; internal async events with sync only where needed, justified.
  4. 2 marksA stateful component with a fitting mechanism (device shadow, checkpointed job), and stateless pods.
  5. 2 marksA coherent k8s plan: zones, namespaces, Deployments, StatefulSets or managed services, CronJob, HPA, rollout.
b.

The route-planning engine runs at 05:00 daily over about 150,000 parcels and needs about 40 minutes of CPU-heavy optimisation. In Phase 2, re-routing must also be triggered by traffic incidents and produce a revised route for the affected couriers within 60 seconds. Would you implement the route planner as serverless functions or as containerised jobs/services on Kubernetes? What are the implications for the rest of the platform?

3 marksA or B, with knock-on effectsCloud nativeDesign principles and when to use serverlessCloud nativeCloud native design on KubernetesCloud nativeHexagonal and serverless architectures
Model answer and marking points

Containers for the daily plan, event-driven for re-routing. A 40-minute, CPU- and memory-heavy optimisation exceeds typical function time limits and is cheaper and more predictable as a Kubernetes Job on a dedicated node pool. Phase 2 re-routing is small, frequent and triggered by events, so it can be a long-running containerised service consuming TrafficIncident events. Serverless is defensible only if each re-route stays well inside the function limits and cold starts fit the 60 s budget. We give up scale-to-zero for the batch.

Implications: the planner needs a consistent snapshot of the day's parcels and stops, taken at a cut-off and exported to the object store. Re-routing needs a live, partitioned stream of incidents and courier positions. Route updates must be versioned, so a courier's app can tell an old route from a new one, and delivered to couriers by push. Delivery must accept route revisions idempotently.

Marking points

  1. 1 markDecision justified by runtime, resource and limit facts (40 min CPU vs function limits; event-triggered re-route).
  2. 1 markTrade-off named: scale-to-zero vs cost predictability, cold starts.
  3. 1 markKnock-on effects: data snapshot or cut-off, event inputs, route versioning, push to couriers, idempotent updates.

Question 3 Total: 12 marks

Refer to the logical architecture in Section 6, the domain model in Section 7 and the logical packaging cum deployment model in Section 8 of the case study, and answer the following questions:

a.

Evaluate the incomplete and potentially erroneous logical packaging cum deployment model for the Deposit Parcel in Locker use case (Figure 3). Identify two undesirable architectural decisions, propose corrective actions and justify how each decision leads to your correction. You may draw only the parts of the diagram needed to illustrate your answer.

6 marksCritique and correct an artefactArchLogical architecture: deployment, verify, validate, reviewArchSecure design principles and reference architecturesArchAccess control and network segregationCloud nativeData store, CQRS and saga patterns
Model answer and marking points

Any two of the following, each as decision → why it is undesirable → correction:

1. Locker Control (CompartmentController) is deployed to the courier's mobile device. The courier app would decide which compartment opens and could unlock by itself, so a tampered or stolen phone can open any compartment. The trust boundary is in the wrong place and complete mediation is lost. Correction: deploy Locker Control on the Locker Controller node, where it accepts only commands authorised by the Locker service (signed, per-compartment, short-lived). The courier app sends a deposit request to Delivery, not an unlock command.

2. PickupCode is manifested by Delivery Manager Data Access. Codes are a LockerManager concern, used at the locker. Storing them in Delivery's database splits ownership, needs cross-service reads at collection time and widens the exposure of secrets. Correction: PickupCode moves to Locker Manager Data Access, stored hashed, and is issued by the Locker service. Delivery holds only a reference.

3. Notification Control (PickupNotifier) is deployed to the Locker Controller. Every locker would need SMS gateway credentials, and codes cannot be sent while the locker is offline. Correction: keep notification server-side, as a function triggered by ParcelDeposited.

4. The Locker Controller has no local data access element, yet Section 4 requires 4 hours of offline collection. Correction: add a Locker Local Data Access subsystem holding hashed or signed codes and a store-and-forward event queue.

Marking points

  1. 3 marksFirst decision: what is wrong in the figure, the consequence (security, ownership, offline), and a concrete corrected packaging or deployment.
  2. 3 marksSecond decision: the same three elements, justified against the case requirements.
b.

Locker operations must reach 99.95% monthly availability (Section 4). In the prototype, a collection depends in series on: the locker controller (99.9%), the site's single 4G router (99.5%), the API gateway (99.95%), the Locker service (99.9%) and its database (99.95%). Compute the current availability of a collection and the monthly downtime it implies. Propose a design that meets the target, and identify two side effects the team should monitor.

6 marksDesign under constraints, then side effectsArchAvailability calculations: nines, series and parallelArchRedundancy: hardware, server, software and dataArchAvailability patterns for web applications
Model answer and marking points

Now: A = 0.999 × 0.995 × 0.9995 × 0.999 × 0.9995 ≈ 0.9920 (99.2%), about 0.008 × 730 h ≈ 5.8 hours of downtime a month. The target, 99.95%, allows about 22 minutes. The 4G router is the weakest link, and the controller alone (99.9%) is already below target.

Design:

  1. Take the network and cloud off the collection path: validate codes locally (signed or hashed codes cached on the controller) and report asynchronously. Collection then depends only on the locker site.
  2. Make the controller redundant: a second controller in active/standby with automatic failover. In parallel, 1 − (0.001)² ≈ 0.999999.
  3. Make deposits less dependent on the network: dual connectivity (4G plus fixed line or a second carrier). In parallel, 1 − 0.005 × 0.005 = 0.999975. Run the cloud services at least 3 replicas across zones, with a multi-zone database.
  4. Result for collection ≈ the redundant controller pair × local power/mechanics, comfortably above 99.95%. Deposits (which need the cloud) ≈ 0.999975 × 0.9995 × 0.99999 × 0.9995 ≈ 99.9%; if they also must hit the target, queue deposits locally and confirm later.

Side effects to monitor: (1) stale codes: cancellations or expiries reach an offline locker only on the next sync, so track sync age per locker; (2) conflicts on reconnect: double allocation of a compartment, or events replayed twice, so watch reconciliation errors and duplicate-event counts; (3) failover that never triggers, or both controllers acting at once (split brain), so test regularly; (4) higher hardware and connectivity cost per site.

Marking points

  1. 2 marksCorrect series calculation (≈ 99.2%) and downtime (≈ 5.8 h/month) vs the target (≈ 22 min).
  2. 2 marksA design that raises availability where it matters: local validation, redundant controller and connectivity (parallel formula), multi-zone services.
  3. 2 marksTwo relevant side effects with what to monitor (stale codes or sync age, reconciliation conflicts, failover, cost).
