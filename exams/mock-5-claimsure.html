Section A 25 marks

Architecting + Platform

Question 1 Total: 25 marks

Refer to the case study in Appendix A and answer the following questions:

a.

ClaimSure's promise is that a claim is acknowledged in seconds and assessed in minutes, even after a storm.

i.

In a flood-surge rehearsal, Report Accident acknowledgements took up to 40 seconds and many failed with timeouts. The logs show every report calling the legacy policy system synchronously to check cover, and the policy system, capped at 20 transactions per second (Section 4), rejecting calls. Give the most likely cause and one countermeasure.

2 marksDiagnose an observationArchAlgorithmic complexity, data placement and flow controlArchFailure sources in distributed systemsCloud nativeMigration options II: Rearchitect to Encapsulate
Model answer and marking points

Cause: a synchronous dependency on a capacity-capped legacy system sits on the critical path of a surge-prone use case. At 1,400 claims per hour plus adjusters' lookups, bursts easily exceed 20 TPS. Requests queue and then time out at the mainframe, retries add more load, and the acknowledgement, which only needs to record the report, waits for a check it does not need to finish first.

Countermeasure: take the mainframe off the acknowledgement path. Record the report and acknowledge at once, then verify cover asynchronously (an event and a worker that calls the policy system at a throttled rate of at most 20 TPS, with a priority queue). Better still, serve cover checks from a coverage replica kept current by change data capture or a nightly-plus-delta feed behind a policy facade, and call the mainframe only when the replica can't answer.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: a synchronous call to a 20-TPS-capped legacy system on the critical path; surge exceeds the cap, so queueing, timeouts and retries.
  2. 1 markCountermeasure: async verification with throttling, or a replicated coverage cache or CDC via a facade, decoupling the ack from the mainframe.
ii.

A developer proposes storing claim photos as BLOB columns in the claims relational database ‘so that a claim and its photos are always consistent’. You recommend storing photos in an object store and keeping only references in the database. Give two reasons.

2 marksDefend a choice against an alternativeCloud nativeCloud persistence options: file storage typesCloud nativeStorage and networkingArchPlanning disk capacity
Model answer and marking points

1. Volume and database health: a surge day is 8,000 claims × 20 photos × 3 MB ≈ 480 GB of photos per day. In BLOB columns that bloats the database, its backups, replicas and restore time, and pushes megabytes through the database tier on every read. An object store scales to that volume cheaply, and the database stays small and fast for the transactional claim data.

2. Direct, scalable upload and lifecycle: the app can upload straight to the object store with pre-signed URLs, so the surge's photo traffic (about 23 MB/s at peak) never passes through the application tier. Lifecycle rules handle the 7-year retention and tiering to cold storage, and upload events trigger the image pipeline. Consistency is handled by referencing the object key and checksum from the claim record (write the reference after the upload completes, and clean up orphans).

Marking points

  1. 1 markVolume or database impact with the case's numbers (≈ 480 GB/day; backups, replication).
  2. 1 markPre-signed direct upload, lifecycle or retention, events; consistency handled by references.
b.

With reference to the domain model in Section 7, analyse the following issues. For each, identify the most likely cause, propose a countermeasure by revising the domain model, and suggest its major drawback(s), if any.

i.

Whenever the policy system team renames or re-codes a field (for example a new coverage code), the Claims, Fraud and Payment services all break and must be released together.

5 marksIssue → cause → countermeasure → drawbackPlatformDecoupling services with context mappingsCloud nativeMigration options II: Rearchitect to EncapsulatePlatformBounded contexts and ubiquitous language in depth
Model answer and marking points

Cause: the legacy model has leaked into three bounded contexts. PolicyRecord in ClaimManager mirrors POLICY_MASTER field by field (POL_NO, CVG_CD, EXP_DT), and FraudCase and Disbursement associate directly with PolicyRecord. The mainframe's record format is effectively a shared model, so the services conform to it with no translation, and any change ripples through all of them.

Countermeasure: create a Policy bounded context in front of the legacy system with an anti-corruption layer (a facade that encapsulates the mainframe). It translates POLICY_MASTER into a stable published language: a CoverageSummary value object (policyId, coverTypes, excess, validFrom/To, insuredVehicle) and a PayeeAccount reference. ClaimManager stores a coverage snapshot taken at report time. Fraud and Payment ask the Policy context (or consume PolicyChanged events) and never touch POLICY_MASTER fields. Only the ACL changes when the mainframe changes.

Drawbacks: another component to build and run, and every change in the legacy system needs mapping work in the ACL. Extra latency or staleness if the ACL caches data. Snapshots may differ from the live policy after endorsements, which needs a re-check rule before payment.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: a legacy record mirrored (conformist shared model) and referenced directly by three contexts.
  2. 1 markExplains the ripple: field changes break all consumers, so coordinated releases.
  3. 2 marksRevised model: a Policy context with ACL or facade, a published-language CoverageSummary, a snapshot in Claim, other contexts depending on the Policy API or events.
  4. 1 markDrawback: ACL upkeep, latency or staleness, snapshot divergence.
ii.

Auditors cannot reconstruct why a claim was approved. The AI estimate shown to the adjuster at approval time has often been overwritten by a later re-run, and nobody can tell which model or prompt produced it.

4 marksIssue → cause → countermeasure → drawbackCloud nativeEvent sourcing, service mesh and circuit breakerArchAnalyzability: detecting and diagnosing problemsCloud nativeKey applications and use cases
Model answer and marking points

Cause: DamageAssessment is a single mutable entity per claim (0..1), with the estimate, model name and an updatedAt. Every re-run overwrites it. Approval references only the Claim, not the assessment the adjuster actually saw, and nothing records the inputs (which photos, which prompt template, which model version) or the adjuster's reasoning.

Countermeasure: make assessments immutable and versioned. Replace DamageAssessment with AssessmentRun (runId, claimRef, photoRefs, modelId and version, promptTemplateVersion, output estimate, confidence, createdAt), with 1..* runs per claim, append-only. Approval (or a Decision entity) references the specific AssessmentRun it relied on, plus the adjuster, the final amount, any override and a rationale. Publish AssessmentCompleted and ClaimApproved events to an audit log.

Drawback: storage and retention: 7 years of runs, inputs and outputs, possibly with personal data inside prompts, so storage must be minimised and encrypted, and erasure rules made explicit. More entities, and reports must pick the ‘current’ run deliberately.

Marking points

  1. 1 markCause: a mutable single assessment overwritten by re-runs; the approval is not linked to the specific run; no model, prompt or input lineage.
  2. 2 marksRevised model: immutable AssessmentRun history with lineage fields; Approval references the run plus rationale; audit events.
  3. 1 markDrawback: storage or retention and PII handling, complexity.

A security review found that a workshop could read other workshops' claims by changing the workshop parameter.

  1. Identify the design and security problems in these interfaces.
  2. Propose how authorisation should work so that a workshop can only see and act on claims assigned to it.
  3. Reconstruct the table with corrected interfaces.
6 marksCritique and correct an artefactPlatformAPI versioning and security basicsPlatformOAuth vs JWT vs SAML, CORS and payload inspectionPlatformResource naming, HTTP methods and idempotencyCloud nativeApplication, user and access security
Model answer and marking points

1. Problems. Broken object-level authorisation: the API trusts a client-supplied workshop id, so any workshop can enumerate any claim. Excessive data exposure: NRIC, phone and address are returned to workshops that only need the vehicle, damage and photos. Action verbs in URIs (submitQuote), a body on DELETE (which many intermediaries drop) and plain-string responses with no status codes. The workshop identity also arrives as a free input rather than from the credential.

2. Authorisation. Workshops authenticate with OAuth2 (client credentials for the portal, or user tokens for staff), so the token carries the workshop id and scopes (claims:read, quotes:write). Every request derives the workshop from the token, never from parameters. The Claim service checks assignment for each object: claim.assignedWorkshop == token.workshop, otherwise 404 (so existence is not leaked). The gateway validates tokens and scopes, and the service enforces object-level rules (complete mediation). Responses use a workshop-specific projection with no policyholder identity. Every access is audited.

3. Corrected interfaces:

Marking points

  1. 2 marksProblems: object-level authorisation (IDOR), oversharing PII, action URIs or DELETE-with-body or no status codes, identity from input.
  2. 2 marksAuthorisation design: identity and scopes from the token, per-object assignment check in the service, 404 on mismatch, data projection, audit.
  3. 2 marksCorrected table: resource URIs, correct verbs and status codes, token-derived identity, minimal data, versioning, idempotency.
d.

To meet the surge requirement, a team member proposes letting the AI model auto-approve every claim under $2,000 already in Phase 1. Do you agree? Justify your answer.

2 marksAgree or disagree with a teammateCloud nativeLLM vs RAG vs Agents, and what GenAI isArchQuality attributes and the utility treeArchRisk treatment: mitigate, avoid, transfer, accept
Model answer and marking points

Disagree. Decisions must be explainable and auditable for 7 years (Section 5), and straight-through processing is planned for Phase 2 with controls. A model estimate is not a decision: models can be wrong or manipulated (staged photos), and an unreviewed $2,000 payout multiplied by a surge is a material fraud and regulatory risk. The surge problem is throughput, and it is better solved by queueing, prioritisation and pre-filled AI estimates that adjusters confirm quickly. Introduce auto-approval later, behind rules (cover confirmed, low fraud score, model confidence above a threshold), with sampling audits and a human for exceptions.

Marking points

  1. 1 markVerdict tied to the explainability or audit NFR and model or fraud risk.
  2. 1 markOffers the right fix for the real driver (surge throughput) and a controlled path to auto-approval.
e.

A team member proposes the number of LLM tokens consumed per day as the key metric showing the quality of the AI assessment and the claim assistant. Do you agree? If not, what would you measure?

4 marksAgree or disagree with a teammateCloud nativeModel families, value and the GenAI stackPlatformMetricsPlatformAPI as a product and design principles
Model answer and marking points

Disagree. Tokens measure usage and cost, not quality. A verbose, wrong answer consumes more tokens than a short, correct one, and a broken prompt that loops could look like ‘high engagement’.

Quality of the AI estimate: the adjuster acceptance rate (estimate approved without change); the deviation of the AI estimate from the final approved amount (median and p90 error); the override rate by damage type; and the performance on a fixed, labelled evaluation set before every model or prompt change. Quality of the assistant: answers grounded in the cited policy text (groundedness or hallucination rate on an evaluation set), resolution without handover to a human, and user rating. Add latency (estimate within 2 min) and error rate.

Keep tokens as a cost metric against the daily budget, per claim and per feature.

Marking points

  1. 1 markVerdict: tokens are cost or usage, not quality; a counter-example.
  2. 2 marksQuality metrics for the estimate (acceptance, deviation, evaluation set) and the assistant (groundedness, resolution).
  3. 1 markKeeps tokens in their place: cost against budget; adds latency.

Section B 13 marks

Cloud native

Question 2 Total: 13 marks

With reference to the case study in Appendix A, answer the following questions. The policy administration system stays on the insurer's mainframe; everything else becomes cloud native, with GenAI features in Phases 1 and 2.

a.

The pilot architecture is shown below.

Transform it into a hybrid cloud native reference architecture for Phases 1 and 2. Hint: consider microservices, workflow, event-driven and serverless processing, and the GenAI components.

i.

Draw the reference architecture with its essential components and interactions. Which components are essential because the policy system remains on the mainframe?

ii.

How will photos flow from the app to the AI assessment, and how will the internal services communicate? Label this in your diagram and justify the patterns.

iii.

Identify one component that must remember state and propose a state-management mechanism.

iv.

Describe the deployment on Kubernetes, including where the AI components run.

10 marksDesign under constraints, then side effectsCloud nativeHybrid, multi-cloud and reference architecturesCloud nativeMigration options II: Rearchitect to EncapsulateCloud nativeServerless architecture patternsCloud nativeLLM vs RAG vs Agents, and what GenAI isCloud nativeCloud native design on KubernetesArchDefer binding: customisation and workflow engines
Model answer and marking points

i. Components. Apps and portals reach the API gateway (OAuth2, object-level authorisation, rate limits) behind CDN + WAF. Services: Intake (records the accident report and acknowledges), the Claim workflow (orchestrates the lifecycle), Assessment (AI), Fraud, Workshop, Payment, Notification, and in Phase 2 the Assistant (RAG). An event stream carries ClaimReported, PhotosProcessed, AssessmentCompleted and ClaimApproved. The object store holds photos (pre-signed upload, 7-year lifecycle), and upload events trigger functions for thumbnails, OCR and image metadata. An AI gateway sits in front of the vision and LLM endpoint (rate limit, cost cap, redaction, logging). A CronJob purges data past retention. Mainframe essentials: private connectivity (redundant VPN or a dedicated link); a policy facade that encapsulates POLICY_MASTER behind a stable API; a coverage replica fed by change data capture so most reads never reach the 20-TPS system; a throttled request queue for the writes that must reach it; and identity federation with the insurer's directory.

ii. Photos and services. The app asks Intake for pre-signed URLs and uploads straight to the object store. ObjectCreated triggers the image function, which publishes PhotosProcessed. The workflow then asks Assessment to run, and Assessment sends a batch of the best photos per claim through the AI gateway and stores an immutable AssessmentRun. Internally, orchestration by the workflow for the claim lifecycle (explicit steps, human tasks, timeouts) uses commands, while events let Fraud, Notification and analytics react without coupling. Synchronous REST only for user-facing reads. The queue gives the AI calls backpressure.

iii. State. The Claim workflow is long-running (days to weeks, waiting on adjusters, workshops and payments). It keeps its state durably: a workflow engine with a persisted state machine per claim (current step, timers, history), in its own database, so pods are stateless and any instance resumes a claim after a crash. Timers drive SLAs, such as escalating if a workshop hasn't quoted in 48 h. (Alternative: the assistant's conversation state, kept in a TTL store keyed by session.)

iv. Kubernetes. A managed cluster in the approved region across three zones, with namespaces for claims, AI, integration and platform. Deployments with HPAs on requests per second or queue depth, and Intake with a high minimum for surges. The workflow engine runs with its database as a managed service. AI: the hosted vision and LLM endpoint is reached through the AI gateway, which runs in the cluster with private connectivity to the provider. If a model is self-hosted, it gets a dedicated GPU node pool with taints and tolerations, scaled on queue depth. Network policies apply, the facade namespace is the only one allowed to reach the private link, secrets come from the KMS, and delivery uses GitOps with canary releases and evaluation-gated model and prompt changes.

Marking points

  1. 3 marksDiagram: intake, workflow, services, stream, object store with functions, AI gateway and endpoint, RAG (Phase 2), data stores.
  2. 1 markMainframe essentials: private link, facade (encapsulate), CDC coverage replica, throttled queue, identity federation.
  3. 2 marksPhoto flow via pre-signed upload → event → function → assessment; orchestration plus events, justified.
  4. 2 marksStateful component with a durable mechanism (persisted workflow state or timers, or a session store) and recovery.
  5. 2 marksk8s plan incl. AI placement (gateway, GPU pool if self-hosted), scaling signals, isolation of the integration path.
b.

Would you coordinate the claim lifecycle (report → assess → quote → approve → pay) by orchestration with a central workflow, or by choreography with services reacting to each other's events? What are the implications for the services?

3 marksA or B, with knock-on effectsPlatformOrchestration vs choreographyArchDefer binding: customisation and workflow enginesPlatformDistributed transactions and the Saga pattern
Model answer and marking points

Orchestration for the lifecycle. Regulators need to see why and when each step happened (Section 5). The lifecycle has human tasks, timeouts and compensations (withdraw a quote, reverse a payment), and the steps will change as Phase 2 adds straight-through processing. A central workflow makes the process explicit, inspectable and changeable as configuration, deferring the binding to run time. In choreography, the end-to-end flow exists only implicitly in many services' subscriptions, which is hard to audit and to change. The cost is that the orchestrator is a critical component and a possible bottleneck, and it must not swallow the domain logic.

Implications: services expose commands (AssessClaim, RequestQuote, Disburse) with idempotent handlers and report completion. The workflow stores state and timers durably. Compensating actions must exist for each step. Events are still published for loosely coupled consumers (fraud analytics, notifications, reporting), so the two styles coexist.

Marking points

  1. 1 markDecision justified by audit, explainability, human steps or timeouts, changeability.
  2. 1 markTrade-off: orchestrator criticality or coupling vs choreography's implicit flow.
  3. 1 markImplications: idempotent commands, durable workflow state or timers, compensations, events kept for side consumers.

Section C 12 marks

Architecting

Question 3 Total: 12 marks

Refer to the logical architecture in Section 6, the domain model in Section 7 and the packaging model in Section 8, and answer the following questions:

a.

Evaluate the incomplete and potentially erroneous logical packaging cum deployment model for the Assess Damage use case (Figure 3). Identify two undesirable architectural decisions, propose corrective actions, and justify how each decision leads to your correction.

6 marksCritique and correct an artefactArchLogical architecture: deployment, verify, validate, reviewArchKey management and protecting data in motionCloud nativeData security and encryptionArchStructural styles and deployment components
Model answer and marking points

Any two, each as decision → consequence → correction:

1. LLM Client Control (EstimatePrompter) is deployed in the adjuster's browser and calls the LLM provider directly. The provider's API key ships to every browser, where it can be extracted and abused. Photos and claim details go to the provider with no redaction, logging, rate limiting or cost cap, and nothing records which model or prompt produced the estimate, which breaks the audit requirement. Correction: move prompting into the Assessment service behind an AI gateway in the internal zone. Keys stay in the KMS, requests are redacted, rate-limited and logged, and AssessmentRuns are stored. The browser only displays results.

2. Assessment Control uses the mainframe directly (‘«use» direct’). That bypasses the integration tier, couples Assessment to POLICY_MASTER and adds load to a 20-TPS system from an interactive path. Correction: go through the Policy facade (anti-corruption layer) and its coverage replica, via the integration tier.

3. DamageAssessment is manifested by both Claim Data Access and Fraud Data Access. Two owners of the estimate cause divergent copies, which is the audit problem again. Correction: Assessment owns its AssessmentRuns. Fraud receives AssessmentCompleted events or queries the Assessment API.

Marking points

  1. 3 marksFirst decision: problem, consequence (key exposure, no redaction, audit, coupling, ownership) and corrected placement or packaging.
  2. 3 marksSecond decision: problem, consequence and correction, justified.
b.

Use the surge figures in Section 4: 8,000 claims in 24 hours with a peak hour of 1,400 claims, 20 photos of 3 MB per claim, an AI estimate within 2 minutes for 90% of claims, an AI provider limit of 300 requests per minute, about $0.04 per single-image request and a $3,000 daily AI budget. Show whether ‘one AI request per photo’ works at the peak, propose a plan that meets the acknowledgement and estimate targets under the surge, and identify two side effects to monitor.

6 marksDesign under constraints, then side effectsArchWhy and how to plan capacityArchAlgorithmic complexity, data placement and flow controlCloud nativeServerless architecture patternsPlatformRate limiting
Model answer and marking points

Load at the peak hour: 1,400 claims/h ≈ 23 claims/min; photos: 28,000/h ≈ 467/min ≈ 7.8/s, about 23 MB/s of uploads.

One request per photo fails twice: 467 requests/min is over the provider limit of 300/min, so a backlog grows by about 167 requests/min and the 2-minute target is lost within minutes of the peak. Cost: 8,000 × 20 = 160,000 requests × $0.04 ≈ $6,400/day, more than double the $3,000 budget.

Plan:

  1. Acknowledge without waiting: Intake records the report and returns the claim number (< 5 s). Photos go by pre-signed upload straight to the object store, so no AI or mainframe call is on this path.
  2. Reduce the AI work per claim: an upload-triggered function makes thumbnails, removes near-duplicates and selects the best 4–6 views. Assessment then sends one multi-image request per claim: about 23 requests/min at peak, well under 300/min. Assume each request costs about $0.10–0.20 for several images: 8,000 × $0.15 ≈ $1,200/day, within budget.
  3. Control the flow: a claims queue with priority (injury and total-loss first), workers that scale on queue depth, and a token-bucket limiter at about 250/min to keep headroom under the provider cap. Retries use backoff and jitter. A per-day cost guard falls back to manual assessment.
  4. Degrade gracefully: if queue age exceeds about 90 s, low-value or low-priority claims get ‘estimate pending’ and are processed off-peak. The 90% target is met by putting the urgent claims first.

Side effects to monitor: (1) estimate quality from fewer or downscaled photos, so watch the adjuster override rate and deviation by damage type; (2) queue age and backlog for deferred claims (time-to-estimate p90); (3) provider throttling and retry storms (429 rate, retry counts); (4) duplicate processing under at-least-once delivery, so assessments must be idempotent per claim and photo set; (5) storage growth of about 480 GB/day and its lifecycle cost.

Marking points

  1. 2 marksCorrect arithmetic: ≈ 467 req/min > 300 limit; ≈ $6,400/day > $3,000; conclusion that per-photo fails.
  2. 2 marksPlan: async ack and direct upload; per-claim batching or photo selection; queue, priority, rate limiter, autoscaling; graceful degradation within budget.
  3. 2 marksTwo side effects with metrics (quality or override rate, queue age, throttling, duplicates, storage).
