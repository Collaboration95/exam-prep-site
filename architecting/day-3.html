Whole day

A system uses TLS, but an administrator account exports confidential records with no business need. Trace the risk and propose two different kinds of control.
Asset: the confidential records; threat: misuse of privileged access; vulnerability: excessive permissions and no monitoring. Assess likelihood and impact, then treat. Apply least privilege and role-based access (technical, preventive), and audit logging with alerts on bulk exports (technical, detective), backed by regular access reviews (administrative, detective). TLS protects data in transit; it does not decide who should read the records.
A telehealth service runs video consultations and stores consultation notes. TLS terminates at a load balancer in the DMZ, clinicians log in with a password, and a vendor supports the video servers remotely. Identify the top three risks and choose a control for each, stating its type and category.
First, stolen clinician passwords give access to patient notes (spoofing, authenticity). Control: MFA (technical, preventive). Second, plaintext at the DMZ load balancer exposes notes if it is compromised (information disclosure). Control: encrypt the notes for the back end, or terminate TLS deeper, and harden the load balancer (technical, preventive). Third, the vendor's remote access becomes a stepping stone. Control: an MFA-protected, logged jump host on a segregated admin network (technical, preventive and detective). Add IDS and log review so the design is not only preventive, and record the residual risk.

Design Secure Architecture

A pharmacy chain's online prescription portal serves 200,000 patients. The web tier in the DMZ reads a records database, a vendor has remote support access, and the budget covers three new controls this year. (a) Identify a crown jewel and a stepping stone. (b) Write two risk scenarios and rate them. (c) Choose a treatment for each and three controls, justifying the mix of types and categories. (d) State the residual risk.
(a) Crown jewel: patient prescription records. Stepping stones: the DMZ web server and the vendor's remote access path. (b) 'An attacker exploits the vendor's remote access to reach the records database and disclose prescriptions': medium likelihood, high impact. 'An attacker exploits a web-tier flaw and pivots to the database': medium likelihood, high impact. (c) Mitigate both. Controls: MFA-protected, logged vendor access through a jump host (technical, preventive and detective); segmentation with an inner firewall that allows only required web-to-database traffic (technical, preventive); centralised log review with alerting (administrative and technical, detective). (d) Residual: unknown application flaws and insider misuse. Record them, accept them within tolerance, and revisit next year.
Threat-model a mobile banking transfer: phone app, then API gateway, then transfer service, then core banking system. For each of three trust boundaries, give the most likely STRIDE threat, the objective it breaks and a control.
Phone to gateway: spoofing (authenticity), using stolen credentials. Control: MFA and device binding. Gateway to transfer service: tampering (integrity), by altering the amount or payee in transit. Control: TLS on each hop plus signed transfer requests, which also give non-repudiation. Transfer service to core banking: elevation of privilege (authorisation), through an over-privileged service account. Control: least privilege and authorising each operation. Across all three, protected audit logs counter repudiation.
A bank's control list for online banking is: TLS, a web application firewall, MFA, a password policy and data-centre locks. An auditor calls it unbalanced. Classify the controls, find the gaps and fill them.
TLS, the web application firewall and MFA are technical and preventive. The password policy is administrative and preventive. The locks are physical and preventive. Everything prevents; nothing detects or corrects. Add detective controls (an IDS, log review, CCTV in the data centre) and corrective ones (tested backups, incident response, access revocation). Check every major risk against a grid of type (technical, administrative, physical) by category (preventive, detective, corrective).
Design how a web login protects a password from browser to the final consumer, when TLS terminates at a DMZ proxy. Discuss the limits of TLS.
TLS protects each connection between its own endpoints, so if it ends at the DMZ proxy, the password is plaintext there even if the next hop also uses TLS. To protect it through that intermediary, encrypt the password field for the final consumer with its public key, plus a nonce that is checked against replay. Protect that private key. Use authenticated ephemeral key agreement for forward secrecy on each connection. Any inspection proxy that decrypts traffic sees plaintext, so include it in the trust boundary.
A small company runs its app server and database on one flat network. The app server's account is a database superuser, admins use their everyday laptops to manage servers, and permissions are cached at login for a day. Apply the four secure design principles.
Defence in depth: add zones (DMZ, internal) with firewalls between them, encryption of sensitive data, and an IDS, so one breach doesn't expose everything. Least common mechanism: give administration and logging their own out-of-band network and dedicated admin devices, and use separate keys per purpose. Least privilege: the app server account gets only the table reads and writes it needs, never superuser. Complete mediation: check authorisation on every request instead of caching it for a day, so revocations take effect immediately. Each fix adds cost or latency, so prioritise them by the risks they reduce.
In a DMZ-based architecture, decide where you would use an IDS and where an IPS, and how you would handle false positives.
Put an inline IPS at the internet edge, where blocking obvious attacks is worth the occasional false block. Place NIDS sensors at internal zone boundaries, such as between the DMZ and the internal network, so they alert without blocking business traffic, and HIDS on critical hosts like the database, which encrypted traffic would otherwise hide. Tune signatures, start new rules in alert-only mode, and review alerts. A passive IDS sees copied traffic and cannot stop an attack, so pair it with a response process.
