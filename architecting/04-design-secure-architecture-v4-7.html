Design Secure Architecture

198 slides · about 84 min · open the deck · JSON

This topic shows how to build security into a software architecture. It starts with the CIA triad and a risk management process (identify assets, threats and vulnerabilities, estimate likelihood and impact, then mitigate, avoid, transfer or accept), then moves to security controls, how they are classified, and the technical controls for resisting, detecting, reacting to and recovering from attacks. It closes with secure design principles and reference architectures. For the exam, expect to assess risk for a scenario, prescribe and classify controls, and justify them with the principles.

Big ideas

  • CIA: confidentiality, integrity, availability; also non-repudiation, authenticity, authorization, accountability.
  • A threat exploits a vulnerability to harm an asset. Write the consequence as a risk scenario; “threat + vulnerability = risk” is a mnemonic, not a numeric formula.
  • Risk = function(likelihood, impact); DREAD scores five factors 1-10 and a 3x3 matrix gives Low/Medium/High.
  • Four risk responses: mitigate (controls), avoid, transfer (reduces impact), accept (within tolerance).
  • Controls have two classifications: type (technical, administrative, physical) and category (preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent, compensating).
  • Technical controls: resist (cryptography, access control, network segregation), detect (signatures, HMAC, IDS), react (revoke, IPS, block exfiltration), recover (tested offline WORM backups, golden images).
  • Cryptography: symmetric ciphers protect bulk data; public-key techniques support key agreement and signatures. Hashes need a trusted comparison value; HMAC uses a shared secret. TLS protects only the connection between its endpoints.
  • Principles: defence-in-depth, least common mechanism, least privilege, complete mediation.

Security concepts: the CIA triad

Security means preserving confidentiality, integrity and availability of information and systems, and it must be designed in from requirements, not bolted on.

  • Secure by design: security requirements must be captured during requirements definition and the solution architecture must be reviewed from a security perspective. Security is a non-functional requirement that shapes the architecture, handled through risk management (assess, then treat).
  • CIA triad: confidentiality prevents unauthorised disclosure; integrity protects data and processing from improper modification; availability keeps required systems and information accessible.
  • Banking example: authentication and access control restrict who can read statements (confidentiality); a $100 payment must remain $100 (integrity); the service must be reachable when needed (availability). Logging in alone does not protect every path to the data.
  • ISO 27000 describes information security as preserving CIA. Other relevant properties include authenticity, accountability, non-repudiation and reliability. Security therefore needs ongoing assessment and operation.
  • Beyond CIA: non-repudiation, authenticity, authorisation and accountability also matter. STRIDE links threat types to CIA, authenticity, non-repudiation and authorisation; audit evidence supports accountability.
Confidentiality
Information is not disclosed to anyone without a valid need to know.
Integrity
Data and processing stay accurate and are not improperly modified.
Availability
Systems and data can be accessed when required.
Information security (ISO 27000)
Preservation of confidentiality, integrity and availability of information.

Check yourself

In a hospital lab system: (a) a nurse views results for a patient not in her care; (b) a script changes a stored potassium value; (c) ransomware locks the system for 6 hours. Classify each against CIA and say which you would protect hardest.
(a) Confidentiality, (b) integrity, (c) availability. Integrity is arguably the most dangerous here, because a silently wrong value leads to wrong treatment, while availability matters for emergencies. Rank them per asset through risk assessment, rather than assuming confidentiality always comes first.
A team says: 'We passed a penetration test at launch, so the system is secure.' Do you agree?
No. Security means preserving confidentiality, integrity and availability over time, while threats, vulnerabilities and the system itself keep changing. A test at launch is one snapshot. Keep assessing and treating risk as an ongoing loop, and repeat testing after significant changes.
Security is added after the architecture is built, by placing a firewall in front of the system. What problems does this cause?
The firewall protects only the network path it sits on. Data at rest, insider access and every other path to the data stay unprotected, and logging in alone does not secure all of them. Controls such as zoning, encryption and access control shape the architecture, so retrofitting them costs more than capturing security requirements and reviewing the design from the start.

Risk management process and TVR

Risk management is an ongoing loop of scoping, assessing (identify, analyse, evaluate) and treating risk; risk arises when a threat meets a vulnerability.

  • The process (ISO 27005 / ISO 31000): determine scope, then risk assessment made of risk identification, risk analysis and risk evaluation, then risk treatment. It is on-going, not a one-off.
  • Identification finds assets, threats, vulnerabilities and consequences. Analysis estimates likelihood and impact. Evaluation uses those results to determine risk levels and priorities against the chosen criteria.
  • TVR mnemonic: a threat is a potential cause of harm; a vulnerability is a weakness it could exploit; risk is the resulting potential loss or damage. A vulnerability need not already be publicly known, and a threat need not be a new incident.
  • Controls reduce risk; they need not remove it. Locking a bicycle reduces theft risk but does not make theft impossible. A data diode restricts a connection to one direction, reducing two-way connectivity exposure; evaluate the remaining paths and risks.
Risk assessment
Identification, analysis and evaluation of risk.
Risk treatment
Choosing and applying a response to each evaluated risk.

Check yourself

A team buys a web application firewall before identifying any assets or threats, then asks 'Are we safe now?' What did they skip, and what is the consequence?
They skipped scoping and risk assessment (identification, analysis, evaluation) and went straight to treatment. They cannot tell whether the control addresses their highest risks, or what residual risk remains. Go back, assess, and then judge whether the firewall was the right treatment, as part of an ongoing process.
A threat report says ransomware gangs are targeting the logistics sector. Your warehouse system is isolated, fully patched and has offline backups. Is your risk high?
Not necessarily. Risk needs a threat, an exploitable vulnerability, an asset and a harmful consequence. Patching and isolation reduce the vulnerability, and offline backups reduce the consequence, so the threat alone does not make the risk high. Some residual risk remains (unknown flaws, insiders), and the assessment must be repeated as conditions change.
To protect a plant's control network, an engineer installs a data diode so data can only flow out to the corporate network. Has the risk been eliminated?
No, it has been reduced. The diode removes the inbound network path, but other paths still need evaluating: maintenance laptops, removable media and vendor access. Controls reduce risk; they need not remove it, so record the residual risk.

Assets, threats, vulnerabilities and risk scenarios

Risk identification: find crown jewels and stepping stones, enumerate threats with STRIDE, spot vulnerabilities, and write each risk as a scenario.

  • Crown jewels vs stepping stones: crown jewels are assets critical to business objectives and what attackers seek (customer account data of a bank, patient records of a hospital). Stepping stones are resources attackers take over to pivot across network segments on the way to the crown jewels. The SingHealth breach is the class case.
  • MOST critical data: out of all data held by the company, identify the small set of most critical data and have a custodian responsible for it, so protection is focused where it matters.
  • STRIDE maps each threat to the objective it breaks: Spoofing -> Authenticity; Tampering -> Integrity; Repudiation -> Non-repudiation; Information disclosure -> Confidentiality; Denial of service -> Availability; Elevation of privilege -> Authorization. Start with STRIDE, then think like a hacker, focusing on data stores, data flows, processes, actors and trust boundaries.
  • Non-repudiation provides evidence of an action, origin or receipt so it can be challenged with proof. It requires the relevant evidence; it does not make denial physically impossible. DoS disrupts service; DDoS uses distributed sources.
  • Vulnerability is a flaw that a threat can exploit to harm an asset (see CWE for catalogues). In other words, your design is vulnerable when it fails to protect CIA, to do AAA (authentication, authorization, accounting) properly, or to provide non-repudiation where required.
  • Write a risk scenario: threat exploits vulnerability to harm asset, causing consequence. Example: an attacker exploits excessive access permissions to read unencrypted medical records, disclosing patient information. Another: ransomware exploits an unpatched system to encrypt business data and stop operations.
Crown jewels
Assets critical to business objectives that attackers actively seek.
Stepping stones

STRIDE
Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, DoS, Elevation of privilege.
Non-repudiation
Evidence of an action, origin or receipt that supports proving it occurred.
Risk scenario
Threat exploits vulnerability to harm asset resulting in consequence.

Check yourself

Attackers compromise a clinic receptionist's PC, which holds no patient data. Why should the incident team treat this seriously?
The PC is a stepping stone: attackers use footholds like this to pivot across network segments to the crown jewels, here the patient records (the SingHealth pattern). Contain it as if the records were at risk, and reduce the number of reachable paths from workstations through segmentation and least privilege.
A mobile banking API returns full account details for any account number once the caller is logged in. Write the risk scenario and name the STRIDE category.
An authenticated attacker (threat) exploits the missing per-account authorisation check (vulnerability) to read other customers' account data (asset), causing disclosure of confidential financial data (consequence). STRIDE: information disclosure, which breaks confidentiality; reaching data beyond one's rights is also elevation of privilege. The fix is to authorise every request against the specific account.
Customers claim they never placed certain large orders, and the logs cannot show otherwise. Which STRIDE threat is this, which objective is broken, and what would you add?
Repudiation, which breaks non-repudiation. Add evidence that ties each action to an authenticated identity, such as digitally signed transactions or protected audit logs. Non-repudiation provides proof to challenge a denial; it does not make denial impossible.
A company holds 400 data sets and has a small security budget. How should it focus its protection?
Identify the small set of most critical data (the crown jewels) and give each one a custodian responsible for it. Protect those, and the stepping stones that lead to them, first. Spreading the budget evenly across all 400 would leave the data that matters most under-protected.

Risk analysis and evaluation: DREAD and the matrix

Each risk is scored for likelihood and impact (DREAD) and combined to give a risk level, often read from a 3-by-3 matrix.

  • Risk = function(Likelihood, Impact). Likelihood is the chance the threat exploits the vulnerability to harm the asset; impact is the magnitude of harm if it occurs. Risk is the likelihood of a threat exploiting a vulnerability to harm an asset, with a business impact.
  • DREAD scores five factors, each 1 to 10: Discoverability (how easy to find the flaw; 10 = public, findable by search engine), Reproducibility (how easy to repeat; 10 = just a browser, no authentication), Exploitability (effort needed; 10 = just a browser, 1 = custom tools and superuser), Affected users (1 none, 5 some, 10 all), Damage potential (1 nothing, 5 individual user data, 10 complete system or data destruction).
  • Interpret DREAD as a scoring aid. Damage and affected users describe impact; discoverability, reproducibility and exploitability inform likelihood. The lecture labels the framework both quantitative and qualitative: its 1–10 scores rank concerns but are not measured probabilities.
  • The lecture’s example 3-by-3 matrix maps probability and impact to Low, Medium or High. In that particular matrix only high/high is High and the low-probability row is Low. This is a chosen scoring policy, not a universal rule that rare catastrophic events are acceptable.
DREAD
Damage, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected users, Discoverability: five 1-10 risk scoring factors.
Likelihood
Chance a threat exploits the vulnerability to harm the asset.
Impact
Magnitude of harm when the threat event occurs.

Check yourself

Flaw A has a public exploit, needs only a browser and no login, and exposes one user's profile. Flaw B needs an insider administrator and custom tools, but can destroy the whole database. Score them roughly with DREAD and say which you would fix first.
A: discoverability, reproducibility and exploitability near 10, affected users and damage low to medium, so high likelihood and moderate impact. A manager wants every risk reported to the board as a single DREAD number. A colleague prefers a 3-by-3 likelihood and impact matrix. Which would you use, and why?
Use both, for different audiences. DREAD's factor scores help the technical team rank and compare flaws. The matrix gives a quick low, medium or high rating that is easier to communicate. Neither is a measured probability, so state the scoring criteria behind them.
A rare event could destroy all patient records. Does a Low matrix cell settle the treatment decision?
No. The matrix uses chosen scoring criteria. Review the severe consequence and organisational tolerance, then decide treatment and record residual risk rather than treating the colour as automatic acceptance.

Risk treatment: mitigate, avoid, transfer, accept

For each risk scenario choose one of four responses; mitigation is where security controls come in.

  • Four responses to cyber risk: Mitigate (apply security controls to reduce the risk), Avoid (do not carry out the activity that gives rise to the risk), Transfer (share part of the risk with another party), Accept (live with it). The choice is made per risk scenario.
  • Mitigation puts in measures to reduce the risk level, via security controls. E.g. a firewall restricting traffic mitigates the risk of a system talking to malicious external servers.
  • Avoidance removes the activity: the Coca-Cola example in class illustrates avoiding an IT risk altogether.
  • Transfer shares part of the risk, for example through insurance or a service contract. It can reduce the financial impact borne by the organisation; it does not erase operational consequences or accountability.
  • Accept means making an informed decision to take no further treatment when risk is within tolerance. Record the decision and revisit it when conditions change. Residual risk remains even after other treatments.
  • Controls definition: after mitigation is chosen, the example scenario (cleartext medical records) is treated with encryption and access control, layered as defence-in-depth. Controls fall into resisting, detecting, reacting and recovering categories (covered later).
Risk tolerance
Level of risk an organisation is willing to accept without further action.
Residual risk
Risk remaining after treatment.
Risk transference
Sharing part of the risk with others, e.g. insurance or outsourcing.

Check yourself

A retailer buys cyber insurance, outsources card processing, and then declares card-data risk 'handled'. Do you agree?
Only partly. Insurance transfers part of the financial impact, and outsourcing reduces the card data the retailer holds. Neither removes operational disruption, reputational harm or the retailer's accountability, and some residual risk stays in-house. Record that residual risk and decide whether it is within tolerance.
An internal wiki with little traffic has an unpatched minor flaw. The fix needs a $50,000 platform upgrade. When could you accept the risk, and what must you record?
Accept it if assessment shows the risk falls within the organisation's tolerance, for example low impact and no access from outside. Record the decision, its owner and the conditions that would trigger a review, such as the wiki starting to hold sensitive data. A cheap compensating control, such as restricting access, may reduce the risk further.
A bank's 'save card for later' feature on a niche product creates serious exposure and earns little revenue. Mitigate or avoid?
Avoid: dropping the feature removes the activity that creates the risk, at little business cost. Mitigation with encryption and access control would add ongoing cost and still leave residual risk. If the feature mattered commercially, mitigation would be the better choice.

Security controls: definition, hygiene and baselines

A security control is the safeguard prescribed to mitigate a risk; in practice you choose controls through risk assessment, hygiene lists or a baseline catalogue.

  • Definition (NIST): a safeguard or countermeasure prescribed for an information system or organisation to protect the CIA of its information and meet defined security requirements. Examples: security guard, CCTV, anti-virus, backup and restore, password, security policy. Controls are to cyber risk what internal controls are to the finance process.
  • Select controls to address requirements and risks. A large catalogue offers choices, not a reason to implement every item. Apply mandatory requirements, assess the relevant risks and justify the selected controls.
  • Cyber hygiene establishes a foundation. The lecture’s CSA Essentials 6 cover assets, authorised software, patching, restricted admin rights, breach detection and access control including MFA. CIS Controls is another prioritised set. Such lists complement risk assessment; they do not establish a percentage probability of being safe.
  • Baselines prescribe a starting set of controls, informed by catalogues such as NIST 800-53 and processes such as NIST 800-37. The lecture distinguishes mandatory, comply-or-explain and optional controls. Apply the organisation’s actual baseline and document justified exceptions.
  • Takeaway: security controls are the solution to the cyber risk problem; choose them via risk assessment, reinforced by hygiene and baselines.
Security control
Safeguard or countermeasure to protect CIA and meet security requirements.
Cyber hygiene

Check yourself

A consultant hands a start-up the full catalogue of about 1,000 security controls and says to implement them all. How do you respond?
No. Controls have costs and limits on where they apply. Start with mandatory obligations and any required baseline, then use risk assessment to select and justify additional controls. A catalogue offers choices, not a checklist to complete.
A 20-person firm with no security staff asks where to start. Risk assessment or a hygiene list?
Start with cyber hygiene, such as the CSA Essentials (asset inventory, authorised software, patching, restricted admin rights, breach detection, access control including MFA). It builds a foundation without a full assessment of each risk. Add risk assessment for the systems holding its crown jewels. Hygiene complements risk assessment; it does not prove the firm is safe.
The baseline mandates MFA for all administrator access on a comply-or-explain basis. A legacy controller cannot support MFA. What do you do?
Explain: document why the control cannot be applied and what alternative reduces the risk, such as reaching the controller only through an MFA-protected jump host on a segregated admin network. Get the exception approved and set a review date. Simply ignoring the control is not an option under comply-or-explain.

Classifying controls: types and categories

Every control has two classifications: what it is made of (technical, administrative, physical) and what it does (preventive, detective, corrective, ...).

  • Two independent questions: form (technical, administrative, physical) and function (preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent, compensating). Encryption is technical and preventive; a log review procedure is administrative and detective. A control can serve more than one function.
  • Every control has both labels. A security policy, for example, is an administrative and preventive control. Encryption is technical and preventive; an IDS is technical and detective; backups are technical and corrective; segmentation instead of patching is technical and compensating.
  • Labels vary between sources. Some materials swap 'types' and 'categories', or add directive and operational/managerial rows. What matters is that controls have two classifications, whatever they are called.
  • Use classification to align the solution to the business: check you have a mix of control forms and functions (not only preventive) for each risk. Applied in the Kopitiam card case, fill a grid of physical/technical/administrative against preventive/detective/corrective to see which are missing.
Preventive
Stops the incident from happening.
Detective
Identifies that an incident is occurring or has occurred.
Corrective
Limits damage and restores after an incident.
Deterrent
Discourages attackers.
Compensating

For a card-payment kiosk, classify each control by type and category: tamper-evident seals, a quarterly log review, an IDS, and restoring from backup.
Tamper-evident seals: physical, detective (and deterrent). Quarterly log review: administrative, detective. IDS: technical, detective. Restore from backup: technical, corrective. Three of the four are detective, so check that preventive controls also exist.
A design's control list is firewall, encryption, MFA and a security policy. What is wrong with this mix?

A legacy system cannot be patched, so the team isolates it on its own segment. Which category?
Compensating: it substitutes for the missing primary control (patching) to still reduce the risk.

Technical controls: cryptography primitives

Resisting attacks on the confidentiality and integrity of data relies on cryptography: symmetric and asymmetric encryption, hashing, HMAC, digital signatures and certificates.

  • Choose the primitive for the security objective. Cryptography includes encryption, hashes, HMAC, signatures, certificates and key management; access control and network segregation provide additional protection.
  • Symmetric encryption uses a shared secret and is efficient for bulk confidentiality. Asymmetric cryptography uses public/private key pairs for operations such as key establishment, authentication and signatures. The lecture’s large speed difference illustrates relative cost, not a fixed multiplier for every algorithm and machine.
  • Encryption: plaintext plus a key and algorithm produces ciphertext. For public-key confidentiality, encrypt to the recipient’s public key; the recipient decrypts with the private key. Signing and verification are separate operations, not a generic instruction to encrypt with either key. Encryption alone does not establish integrity.
  • Hashing: a one-way function produces a fixed-length digest. Comparing with a trusted digest helps detect changes; an attacker who can replace both the data and digest defeats an unkeyed check. Different inputs can collide, so not every change is guaranteed a distinct hash. The lecture contrasts MD5 with SHA-2 and SHA-3; CRC and check digits detect errors rather than malicious tampering.
  • HMAC combines a shared secret with hashing to detect modification and authenticate a key holder. Anyone holding that secret can create the tag, so it cannot distinguish one holder from another for non-repudiation.
  • Digital signatures use a private signing key and public verification key to support integrity, origin authentication and non-repudiation evidence. A certificate binds a public key to a subject under an issuer’s signature. Check the issuer and certificate before trusting the claimed identity; Netrust is the lecture’s CA example.
  • Correct use matters: administrators configure TLS/FTP-SSL endpoints, developers use crypto libraries correctly, and keys must be protected. A strong algorithm does not repair weak key handling or an incorrect trust decision.

Hash function
One-way fixed-length digest; useful for integrity checking when the expected digest is trusted.

Check yourself

A team encrypts a 2 GB nightly backup directly with the recipient's RSA public key. Critique it and propose a better scheme.
Public-key operations are relatively costly and suit key establishment, not bulk data. Encrypt the backup with a random symmetric key, then protect that key with the recipient's public key, so only the private-key holder can recover it. Protect the private key well, because it now unlocks every backup.
Choose a mechanism for each: (a) internal services that share a secret must detect tampered messages; (b) a bank must later prove that a customer authorised a transfer; (c) users download an installer and compare it with a digest published on the same website, which may be compromised.
(a) HMAC: shared-secret integrity and authentication of a key holder. (b) A digital signature with the customer's private key, because HMAC cannot provide non-repudiation when both sides hold the secret. (c) A digest from the same compromised site is worthless, since the attacker can replace both files. Use a signature verified with a trusted public key, or a digest from a trusted channel.
A client accepts any certificate whose subject matches the server's name, without checking who issued it. What attack does this allow?
An attacker can present their own certificate with the same subject name and impersonate the server (spoofing, then reading or altering traffic). A certificate binds a key to a subject only through the issuer's signature. Verify that signature up to a trusted CA before trusting the identity.
An attacker can rewrite a file and its stored SHA-256 digest. Has the digest protected integrity?
No. The attacker can recompute an unkeyed digest. Use a trusted comparison value, an HMAC with a protected shared key, or a verified digital signature, according to the trust model.

Key management and protecting data in motion

Keys must be generated, stored and agreed safely (HSM, random generators, Diffie-Hellman), and TLS and VPNs use them to protect data in transit.

  • Protect keys as well as data. The lecture recommends HSMs for long-term keys and random bit generators or authenticated key agreement for symmetric/session keys. It introduces FIPS 140-2 Levels 1–4 and CloudHSM as examples of increasing physical and authentication protection; these examples do not replace a threat assessment.
  • Unpredictable key material matters. The lecture’s seeded Python example shows determinism: the same seed repeats the sequence. Key generation needs a cryptographic generator with adequate entropy, not an ordinary reproducible random sequence. The lecture illustrates entropy sources with HSM noise sources and Cloudflare lava lamps.
  • Forward secrecy: recorded sessions using long-term RSA key transport can be exposed if that private key is later stolen. Authenticated ephemeral Diffie-Hellman derives per-session secrets without transmitting them directly. With ephemeral secrets erased, later theft of the long-term key does not reveal those past sessions; unauthenticated DH alone does not prove peer identity.
  • TLS protects one connection between its endpoints. If it terminates at the DMZ web server, plaintext is available there; a second TLS hop does not remove that exposure. It can be end-to-end when its endpoints are the actual producer and consumer. The lecture illustrates handshake round-trip savings but uses imprecise version labels; do not memorise fixed message counts.
  • Field encryption can extend protection across intermediaries. The lecture illustrates encrypting a password plus nonce for the bank’s final consumer. The nonce addresses replay only when checked appropriately. A proxy that decrypts traffic can inspect plaintext and becomes part of the trust boundary; a diagram alone is not a complete crypto protocol.
  • VPN: an encrypted tunnel over public networks gives confidentiality and integrity; IPsec traditionally, TLS for SSL VPN. Forms: site-to-site and point-to-site; transport vs tunnel mode.
  • VPN placement is a trust-boundary trade-off. DMZ termination exposes the endpoint and keys in the outer zone but permits an inner control boundary. Internal termination keeps keys deeper inside yet may bypass DMZ inspection. Neither placement automatically makes keys safe or grants unrestricted access: authentication, routing and authorisation still matter.
HSM
Tamper-resistant hardware that stores long-term keys and performs crypto (FIPS 140-2 levels 1-4).
PRNG
Deterministic generator: same seed, same output.
Perfect forward secrecy
Ephemeral keys so stealing a long-term key does not expose past sessions.
Diffie-Hellman
Key agreement letting both sides derive a shared key without transmitting it.
TLS
Protocol protecting data in transit between client and server.

Check yourself

An attacker has recorded two years of a server's TLS traffic and now steals the server's long-term private key. Under which key exchange can the past traffic be decrypted?
With RSA key transport, yes: the session keys were sent encrypted with that key, so all the recorded traffic opens. With authenticated ephemeral Diffie-Hellman whose session secrets were erased, no: that is forward secrecy. Prefer ephemeral key agreement, and protect long-term keys in an HSM.
TLS terminates at a load balancer in the DMZ, and traffic to the application server is re-encrypted with a second TLS hop. A teammate says passwords are protected end to end. Do you agree?
No. Each TLS connection protects only between its own endpoints, so the password is plaintext inside the load balancer in the DMZ. If that exposure is unacceptable, encrypt the password field for the final consumer (with a nonce against replay), or terminate TLS at the application server. Otherwise, treat the load balancer as part of the trust boundary and harden it.
A vendor needs remote access to maintain one internal server. Where would you terminate their SSL VPN, and what else must you control?
Terminating in the DMZ keeps the exposed endpoint outside the inner boundary, and the inner firewall then allows only that one server. Terminating internally keeps keys deeper inside but bypasses DMZ inspection. Either way, the VPN gives only a tunnel: you still need strong vendor authentication, authorisation limited to that server, and logging of the session.
A developer generates session keys with the language's standard random function, seeded with the current time. What is the problem?
The sequence is deterministic: the same seed repeats the same output, and an attacker who can guess the time can reproduce the keys. Use a cryptographic random generator with enough entropy, or authenticated key agreement, for session keys.

Access control and network segregation

Access control (identify, authenticate, authorize, with audit and administration) and segregated networks limit who can reach what.

  • Access control chain: Identification (state who you are) -> Authentication (prove it) -> Authorization (who is allowed what type of access to which objects). Audit trail and administration surround it. It is a baseline security control.
  • Authentication factors: know (password/passphrase), have (OTP token), are (physiological or behavioural biometrics such as fingerprint, iris, handwriting or gait). MFA combines independent factor types; two passwords are still one factor type.
  • Segregation limits movement. Split a flat network into zones such as DMZ, internal, user and development, with controlled connections. A compromised host then has fewer reachable targets; permitted paths still need authentication and authorisation.
  • Out-of-band network: a segregated LAN for logging and a dedicated device and segregated network for system administration, so management traffic is separate from business traffic. The class exercise on a vendor using TeamViewer asks you to spot how an easy outbound remote tool can bypass firewall design.
  • Summary of resisting attacks: cryptography, access control and network segregation.
Authentication
Proving the claimed identity.
Authorization
Determining which access an identity has to which objects.
Network segregation
Separating networks into zones with controlled traffic between them.
Out-of-band network
Separate network for logging and administration.

Check yourself

A bank offers three login options: (a) password plus a one-time code from a hardware token; (b) fingerprint plus a key bound to the phone; (c) password plus a security question. Which are true multi-factor?
(a) is multi-factor: something you know plus something you have. (b) is multi-factor: something you are plus something you have. (c) is a single factor, because both are knowledge. MFA needs independent factor types, not just more secrets.
Ransomware on one office PC reached the database servers within an hour. What design flaw does this suggest, and what is the fix?
A flat network: once inside, any host could reach the others. Segregate it into zones (user, DMZ, internal, development) with controlled connections, so a compromised PC has few reachable targets, and move administration to an out-of-band network. Permitted paths still need authentication and authorisation.
A vendor installs an outbound remote-desktop tool on a server so they can support it. The firewall blocks all inbound traffic. Is the server safe from outside access?
No. The tool opens a connection from the inside, which the inbound rules don't stop, and the vendor's service becomes a path into the network. Route vendor access through a controlled, authenticated and logged path, such as a VPN or jump host on the admin network, and block unapproved outbound remote tools.
Does requiring both a password and a PIN give two-factor authentication?
No. Both are knowledge factors. Combine independent types, such as a password with possession of an OTP token, to obtain multiple factors.

Detecting, reacting and recovering

Beyond resisting attacks, technical controls detect them (IDS, signatures, HMAC), react to them (revoke, IPS, block exfiltration) and recover (tested, protected backups).

  • Detecting attacks: detect data tampering with digital signatures and HMAC, and detect intrusions as they occur with an IDS. IDS can be on hosts or networks and can use different monitoring methodologies.
  • HIDS vs NIDS: a host-based IDS is installed on each protected system and monitors system calls, file system access, registry changes and host I/O to detect anomalous activity. A network-based IDS has sensors on firewalls and routers and watches network traffic for attacks; placement of sensors is a design choice.
  • IDS versus IPS: a passive network IDS analyses copied traffic and alerts; an inline IPS can drop suspected attacks. Here “offline” means outside the forwarding path, not delayed or disconnected. HIDS observes host activity. Blocking introduces a false-positive and availability trade-off.
  • Reacting to attacks: revoke access, use IDPS to block, and block data exfiltration.
  • Recovery: test restoration and protect backup copies with offline storage, access controls and WORM where appropriate. WORM prevents rewriting retained data; it does not prevent theft or guarantee that the original copy was clean. Golden images provide a known-good base for rebuilding systems.

Host intrusion detection system monitoring a single host.
NIDS
Network intrusion detection system watching traffic via sensors.
IPS
Inline system that drops malicious traffic.
WORM
Write once, read many: retained data cannot be overwritten; other backup protections are still required.
Golden image
Known-good system image for rebuilding.

Check yourself

A patient-records API sits behind an inline IPS that could block attacks, but false positives would also block clinicians. Would you run it in blocking mode or as an alerting IDS?
It depends on the cost of a false block. Here, blocking legitimate clinicians hurts availability for patient care, so start in alerting (IDS) mode, tune the rules, and enable blocking only for well-understood signatures. On a public edge where a false block costs little, inline blocking is easier to justify.
Traffic to an application server is end-to-end encrypted, and you suspect an attacker is modifying files on it. HIDS or NIDS?
A HIDS: it runs on the host and watches system calls, file-system changes and host activity, which encrypted network traffic hides from a network sensor. The cost is an agent to deploy and maintain on each protected host.
Ransomware encrypted both production and the online backups mounted on the same network. What should the backup design have included?
Offline copies, access controls on the backup system, WORM retention where appropriate, regularly tested restores, and golden images to rebuild systems. WORM stops rewriting but not theft, and it doesn't prove the copy was clean, so test restores as well.
An IDS alerts that a staff account is uploading large volumes of data to an unknown external host at 3 am. Which reacting controls apply, and what is the risk of using them?
Revoke or suspend the account's access, block the destination with an IPS or firewall rule, and stop the exfiltration. The risk is a false positive, such as a legitimate overnight transfer, disrupting work. Confirm quickly and keep the response proportionate.

Secure design principles and reference architectures

Four principles (defence-in-depth, least common mechanism, least privilege, complete mediation) guide control choices; reference architectures show them applied.

  • Defence-in-depth: multiple layers of protection so that if one is breached, others still protect, since no single measure is foolproof. E.g. access control plus encryption protecting confidential data.
  • Least common mechanism: minimise protection mechanisms shared across users, because shared paths can be channels for unauthorised information exchange. Examples: segregated logging LAN, dedicated admin device and network, different crypto keys for different purposes, compartmentalised functions per role.
  • Least privilege: give minimum permissions for the minimum time needed. Limits the damage when vulnerabilities are exploited. An application server gets network access, read on one database table and write to a log file, and never superuser.
  • Complete mediation: every access needs a valid authorisation decision. The lecture warns against cached decisions because revoked rights may otherwise remain effective. A successful login does not authorise every later object or operation.
  • Reference architectures: AWS DDoS-resilient design layers Route 53, CloudFront, WAF, API Gateway, load balancing and auto-scaling in front of private-subnet EC2 (defence-in-depth against DoS). Mobile security uses MDM (enforces policy on devices) and MAM (controls specific apps and their data).
  • Summary: security = CIA plus non-repudiation, authenticity, authorization, accountability; risk = function(likelihood, impact); treat with controls and principles. The IROne exercise practises asset, threat/vulnerability pairs and mitigations.

Check yourself

A web app checks the user's role at login and stores 'isAdmin = true' in the session for 8 hours. An administrator is dismissed at 10 am. Which principle is broken, and what is the risk?
Complete mediation. The cached decision keeps the revoked rights working for up to 8 hours. Check every access against current permissions. The cost is a permission lookup per request, which is the price of revocation taking effect immediately.
A service account has superuser rights 'for convenience'. Which principle and what harm?
Least privilege: if the account is exploited the attacker inherits superuser power; grant only network, one table read and log write.
A public API faces DDoS risk, and its only protection is a perimeter firewall. Propose a defence-in-depth arrangement.
Layer the controls: DNS and a content delivery edge to absorb volume, a web application firewall, an API gateway that throttles requests, load balancing with auto-scaling, and application servers in private subnets. Protect the data behind them with access control and encryption. Each layer catches some of what passes the one before. The cost is more components to configure and pay for.
Production and test share one encryption key and one admin jump host 'to keep things simple'. Which principle is broken, and what is the risk?
Least common mechanism: shared mechanisms become channels between environments. Compromising the weaker test environment exposes the production key and the production admin path. Use separate keys per purpose and environment, and a dedicated admin path for production.

Exam-style questions

Answer aloud or on paper before opening.

A pharmacy chain's online prescription portal serves 200,000 patients. The web tier in the DMZ reads a records database, a vendor has remote support access, and the budget covers three new controls this year. (a) Identify a crown jewel and a stepping stone. (b) Write two risk scenarios and rate them. (c) Choose a treatment for each and three controls, justifying the mix of types and categories. (d) State the residual risk.
(a) Crown jewel: patient prescription records. Stepping stones: the DMZ web server and the vendor's remote access path. (b) 'An attacker exploits the vendor's remote access to reach the records database and disclose prescriptions': medium likelihood, high impact. 'An attacker exploits a web-tier flaw and pivots to the database': medium likelihood, high impact. (c) Mitigate both. Controls: MFA-protected, logged vendor access through a jump host (technical, preventive and detective); segmentation with an inner firewall that allows only required web-to-database traffic (technical, preventive); centralised log review with alerting (administrative and technical, detective). (d) Residual: unknown application flaws and insider misuse. Record them, accept them within tolerance, and revisit next year.
Threat-model a mobile banking transfer: phone app, then API gateway, then transfer service, then core banking system. For each of three trust boundaries, give the most likely STRIDE threat, the objective it breaks and a control.
Phone to gateway: spoofing (authenticity), using stolen credentials. Control: MFA and device binding. Gateway to transfer service: tampering (integrity), by altering the amount or payee in transit. Control: TLS on each hop plus signed transfer requests, which also give non-repudiation. Transfer service to core banking: elevation of privilege (authorisation), through an over-privileged service account. Control: least privilege and authorising each operation. Across all three, protected audit logs counter repudiation.
A bank's control list for online banking is: TLS, a web application firewall, MFA, a password policy and data-centre locks. An auditor calls it unbalanced. Classify the controls, find the gaps and fill them.
TLS, the web application firewall and MFA are technical and preventive. The password policy is administrative and preventive. The locks are physical and preventive. Everything prevents; nothing detects or corrects. Add detective controls (an IDS, log review, CCTV in the data centre) and corrective ones (tested backups, incident response, access revocation). Check every major risk against a grid of type (technical, administrative, physical) by category (preventive, detective, corrective).
Design how a web login protects a password from browser to the final consumer, when TLS terminates at a DMZ proxy. Discuss the limits of TLS.
TLS protects each connection between its own endpoints, so if it ends at the DMZ proxy, the password is plaintext there even if the next hop also uses TLS. To protect it through that intermediary, encrypt the password field for the final consumer with its public key, plus a nonce that is checked against replay. Protect that private key. Use authenticated ephemeral key agreement for forward secrecy on each connection. Any inspection proxy that decrypts traffic sees plaintext, so include it in the trust boundary.
A small company runs its app server and database on one flat network. The app server's account is a database superuser, admins use their everyday laptops to manage servers, and permissions are cached at login for a day. Apply the four secure design principles.
Defence in depth: add zones (DMZ, internal) with firewalls between them, encryption of sensitive data, and an IDS, so one breach doesn't expose everything. Least common mechanism: give administration and logging their own out-of-band network and dedicated admin devices, and use separate keys per purpose. Least privilege: the app server account gets only the table reads and writes it needs, never superuser. Complete mediation: check authorisation on every request instead of caching it for a day, so revocations take effect immediately. Each fix adds cost or latency, so prioritise them by the risks they reduce.
In a DMZ-based architecture, decide where you would use an IDS and where an IPS, and how you would handle false positives.
Put an inline IPS at the internet edge, where blocking obvious attacks is worth the occasional false block. Place NIDS sensors at internal zone boundaries, such as between the DMZ and the internal network, so they alert without blocking business traffic, and HIDS on critical hosts like the database, which encrypted traffic would otherwise hide. Tune signatures, start new rules in alert-only mode, and review alerts. A passive IDS sees copied traffic and cannot stop an attack, so pair it with a response process.
