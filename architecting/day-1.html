Day 1 mock questions

Do these after all of today's topics. Give your choice, reason and trade-off before opening an answer.

Whole day

A city's parking-permit system receives about 30,000 applications in the first week of each year and about 200 a week afterwards. It must take a nightly file from the vehicle registry, and two developers will maintain it. Stakeholders propose microservices. Do you agree? Which qualities and styles should drive the choice?
Probably not microservices. With two developers and one product, the operational cost (discovery, gateways, automation) outweighs independent deployment. The driving qualities are elasticity for the January spike and cost the rest of the year, plus reliable file-level integration with the registry. A modular tiered application on an elastic platform, with file-level data flow for the registry feed, meets them more simply. Record the qualities as measurable scenarios in a utility tree, so the choice can be revisited if more teams or functions arrive.
A ticket-resale marketplace runs in two regions. Buyers browse listings, sellers update prices, and a purchase must never sell the same ticket twice. Which operations favour availability during a partition, which messaging style suits notifying sellers, and which views would you give operations and auditors?
Browsing listings and showing prices can favour availability with short staleness. The purchase must favour consistency, waiting or rejecting during a partition, because selling a ticket twice is unacceptable, and an ACID label does not settle partition behaviour. Notify sellers by publish/subscribe, so email, push and analytics subscribe independently. Give operations the physical and deployment views (regions, nodes, failover) and auditors the information-structure and deployment views showing where payment data lives and who can reach it.

Introduction to Solution Architecture

A hospital group's enterprise architecture mandates one identity provider and an approved set of hosting regions. You are solution architect for a new telemedicine app, and a developer has already produced class diagrams and picked libraries. (a) Which decisions here belong to EA, SA and detailed design? (b) The developer's design skips the identity provider to save time. What do you do?
(a) The identity provider and hosting regions are EA decisions: strategic direction that constrains the solution. How the app's components collaborate to meet its qualities, and how it integrates with the identity provider, is solution architecture. Classes and libraries are detailed design, and they must fit inside the SA. (b) Align the design with EA. Bypassing the mandated identity provider breaks an enterprise constraint and creates a separate set of credentials to secure. Redesign the integration, or raise a justified exception with the enterprise architect if a requirement truly cannot be met. Record the decision either way.
A brilliant architect rarely attends team meetings. The team cannot build the design within budget and keeps asking him how two components should interact. Critique the situation and propose remedies.
This combines several failure patterns: an ivory-tower architect, an absent architect during construction, and a 'perfect' architecture that exceeds cost constraints, so the solution is not viable. A good architect earns the team's trust, documents an implementable architecture and its rationale, supports detailed design and construction, and leaves the team able to proceed without him. Remedies: re-evaluate the options against the budget and skills, document the component interactions and decisions, and hold regular design sessions with the team.
A mobile banking app must show balances within 1 s for 95% of requests. Security wants step-up authentication on every screen. (a) Name the quality relationship. (b) Write two utility-tree leaves with plausible ranks. (c) Propose a resolution and say what you give up.
(a) An inverse relationship between security (authenticity, confidentiality) and performance efficiency and usability, under this design. (b) For example: 'balance view returns within 1 s for 95% of requests at peak' (H,M), and 'a transfer above a set amount requires a second factor before it executes' (H,L). (c) Apply step-up authentication to risky operations such as transfers and payee changes, not to every screen, so balance views stay fast. You give up some protection on low-risk screens. The utility-tree ranking makes that trade-off explicit and reviewable.
A flash-sale platform expects 50,000 users in the first 10 minutes, takes payment through one external gateway, and changes its catalogue daily. Build a utility-tree fragment with three leaves, rank them, and say which drives the architecture first.
Leaves, for example: Performance: 'product page under 2 s for 95% of requests at 50,000 users in 10 minutes' (H,H). Availability: 'if the payment gateway times out, orders are held and confirmed within 5 minutes without double charging' (H,H). Modifiability: 'a catalogue change is published in under 1 hour without a deployment' (M,L). The high-importance, high-risk leaves come first: peak performance and payment failure handling shape the structure. The catalogue leaf can be met with known techniques. Evaluate the architecture by whether it makes each high-ranked scenario achievable.
A teammate says the only security requirement for a payments system is confidentiality, so TLS plus encrypted storage is enough. Do you agree? Use the ISO 25010 security sub-characteristics.
Disagree. TLS and encryption mainly address confidentiality and part of integrity. Payments also need integrity of stored amounts, authenticity (the payer is who they claim), non-repudiation (a customer cannot credibly deny a transfer they made) and accountability (each action is traced to one user). Each needs different mechanisms, such as strong authentication, signed or well-audited transactions, and per-user audit logs. Prioritise them with measurable scenarios rather than one blanket control.

Architect the Solution Architecture (Assets)

A retailer's legacy inventory system offers only a nightly CSV export and read access to its database. A new web shop needs stock levels no more than 1 minute old, synchronous order placement, and order notifications to email, SMS and analytics. (a) Choose a communication style and integration level for each need. (b) The inventory team then forbids direct database access. What changes?
(a) Stock: database-level data flow (replication or a view), because 1-minute freshness rules out the nightly file. Orders: application-level call and return through a web service, because the customer needs an immediate result. Notifications: publish/subscribe, so email, SMS and analytics subscribe independently and new channels need no change to the order service. Structurally, use a tiered client-server design with a reverse proxy in the DMZ. (b) Without database access, only the file export remains. Either negotiate an application-level interface on the inventory system or accept stale stock data and show availability as approximate. Document this as a constraint-driven decision with its business impact.
A concert-ticketing platform runs in two regions. During an on-sale for 20,000 seats, the regions lose contact for 2 minutes. Which operations favour availability and which favour consistency? What does the customer see, and does an ACID database settle the question?
Browsing events and seat maps can favour availability and accept briefly stale seat maps. Seat reservation and payment must favour consistency, or two regions could sell the same seat. During the partition, those operations wait or are refused in the region that cannot coordinate, and the customer sees a 'try again' or queue message. Reconcile holds when the link returns. ACID transactions guarantee rules within a transaction, but say nothing about partition behaviour, so the replication design must be checked separately.
A five-year-old monolith: during sales the catalogue gets 50 times the traffic of checkout; three teams work on it; a nightly report reads every table. Propose a decomposition using the scale cube and name two side effects to monitor.
Y axis: split by business capability (catalogue, checkout, accounts), each service owning its data, so teams deploy independently. X axis: clone the catalogue service heavily for sales peaks without scaling checkout. Z axis: partition order data later if it grows too large. Add an API gateway and service discovery. The nightly report can no longer read every table; feed it from events or replicas. Side effects to monitor: cross-service consistency (orders referring to changed catalogue data), and latency and failures across the new network call chains, which need tracing and timeouts.
Compare two organisations. A bank has 15 legacy core systems, and loan approval is a long process orchestrated across six of them. A start-up has one product, two teams and frequent releases. Would you lean towards broker/SOA or microservices for each, and why?
The bank leans towards SOA with a broker: layered services over the existing operational systems, with process orchestration in the integration layer, reusing enterprise services. Make the broker redundant, because it is a single point of failure and a bottleneck risk. The start-up leans towards a modular monolith now and microservices later: independent vertical slices with their own data favour agility, but only once the team can support discovery, gateways and automation. The deciding factors are the number of systems to integrate, the complexity of orchestration and the team's operational maturity.
A new payments platform is reviewed by an auditor (where card data flows and who can reach it), operations (servers, sites, failover) and developers (module structure, build). How would you document the architecture, and how do you keep the views consistent?
Follow 42010: list the stakeholders and their concerns, then choose viewpoints. Auditor: an information-structure view (class or ER model) for card data, plus deployment and infrastructure views showing zones and permitted connections. Operations: the physical or deployment view, with nodes, sites and failover paths. Developers: the development view and an application-structure view (packages, components). Use a process view for throughput and concurrency. Tie the views together with key scenarios such as 'authorise a card payment', and trace each scenario through every view.
An exam-results portal is idle most of the year, then 200,000 students log in during the first hour of results day. The results are personal data under strict regulation. Which reference-architecture options would you weigh, and what do you give up with each?
The demand profile calls for elasticity: capacity that scales up for one hour and back down. That favours public cloud with containers on an elastic platform, or serverless functions for the read-heavy results lookup, which scale without managed servers. Data sensitivity pushes the other way, towards private or hybrid deployment, keeping the results store in a controlled environment and putting only the elastic front end in public cloud. Serverless gives up runtime control, and private hosting gives up elasticity, forcing you to provision for the peak. Judge the options by availability on results day, security, cost and responsiveness under the spike, the Reactive goal.
