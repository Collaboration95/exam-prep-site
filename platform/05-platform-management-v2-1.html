Platform Management

80 slides · about 37 min · open the deck · JSON

Platform management keeps interactions scalable, available and safe to change. This topic covers discovery, deployment strategies, circuit breakers, rate limits, tracing, sagas, metrics and centralised logs. Practise choosing a tool from the failure it prevents and explaining the assumptions behind its guarantees.

Big ideas

  • A platform's value is the producer-consumer interaction; engineering scales, limits, measures and evolves it.
  • Service discovery registers names and locations, resolves a target, then connects. Keep discovery infrastructure available; consensus-backed registries commonly use 3 or 5 nodes.
  • Blue/green switches environments; rolling gradually replaces instances; canary limits live exposure; shadowing mirrors requests and discards new-version responses.
  • Circuit breaker is a state machine (Closed, Open, Half-Open) that fails fast to stop cascading failures; rate limiting caps callers using an in-memory quota store.
  • Distributed tracing passes a UUID via headers; a trace spans services, a span is one unit of work.
  • Sagas replace 2PC with local transactions plus compensations (orchestrated or choreographed) but offer no read isolation.
  • Distinguish time-based uptime, request success (yield) and reachable data (harvest). Use the lecture’s stated uptime formula when requested, with its denominator assumption explicit.
  • Prefer platform features (Kubernetes DNS, Istio, ELK/Prometheus) over hand-coded plumbing.

Why engineers care: the platform toolbox

A platform exists to enable producer-consumer interaction, and engineering's job is a toolbox that scales, protects and evolves that interaction.

Platform = business model; engineering makes it work

  • Creates value by facilitating exchanges between interdependent groups (consumers and producers)
  • Examples →
    • videos on YouTube
    • data and APIs for a health agency
  • Modern platforms blur infrastructure and application architecture
  • So they must solve →
    • service discovery
    • resource coordination
    • container orchestration
    • usage reporting

Engineering is what makes the platform's interactions work.

Three engineering concerns for survival

  • Scalability → increasing and limiting interaction
  • Availability → a seamless experience
  • Evolvability → shipping fixes and features

These decide whether the platform survives: a consistently bad experience kills a platform.

Toolbox maps tools to business intent

  • Service discovery
  • Deployment strategies (canary, rolling)
  • Circuit breaker and rate limiting
  • Distributed tracing
  • Metrics collection
  • Log aggregation

Together they scale the interaction, limit it, measure it, and let you enhance the platform without disrupting users.

A product manager calls deployment strategies and distributed tracing 'ops plumbing' and wants the platform team on features only. How do you argue for them?

A platform's value is the interaction between producers and consumers; a consistently bad experience drives both away. The toolbox protects that interaction:

  • Scalability: increasing and limiting it
  • Availability: a seamless experience
  • Evolvability: releasing fixes and features safely
  • Data to improve it

Map each tool to the business intent it serves: canary releases, rate limiting and distributed tracing.
  • Canary releases → experimentation and seamless rollout = evolvability without disrupting users
  • Rate limiting → limits the interaction, protecting scarce resources and spending from excessive or abusive traffic
  • Distributed tracing → lowers reaction time when something breaks → shortens recovery, protects availability

Service discovery

How services find each other when instances come and go: a registry that works like an internal DNS, kept highly available by a consensus cluster.

Architecture trade-off

  • Monolith → operationally simpler, but scales and deploys as a unit
  • SOA and microservices → add service boundaries and reuse
  • Microservices → favour independent scaling, deployment and data ownership, with greater operational overhead
  • Containers are a common implementation of microservices, not their definition

Why hardcoded IPs fail in the cloud

  • Auto-scaling creates new instances that must join load balancing
  • Nodes or containers restart and move (fallacies of distributed computing)

Requirements for a discovery solution

  • Survive node failures
  • Store service locations
  • Spare clients from using raw IPs
  • Not a single point of failure

Answer: name-based lookup (like DNS)

As on the internet (DNS), look services up by name.

  • Service registry = effectively an internal DNS server
  • East-west traffic → service to service inside the platform (what the registry serves)
  • North-south traffic → users to platform

Service discovery: three steps

  1. Register the service (name to IP)
  2. Look it up from the registry
  3. Connect to it

Keep discovery available

  • Consensus-backed registries → ZooKeeper, etcd, Consul
  • Commonly use 3 or 5 nodes
  • A majority must agree on changes
  • 3 nodes tolerates one unavailable node; 5 nodes tolerates two
  • Even-sized clusters can form a majority, but an extra even-numbered member does not increase failure tolerance

Use platform discovery

  • Kubernetes (lecture example) → callers use a service alias such as http://payment-manager-service:8080
  • In Kubernetes, service registration and DNS integration are platform-managed
  • Spring Cloud/Eureka example → requires more application integration

Prefer a supported platform capability over duplicating plumbing.

East-West traffic
Traffic between services inside the platform.
North-South traffic
Traffic between users and the platform.
Service registry
Internal DNS-like store of service names and locations.
Consensus protocol
Paxos/Raft/ZAB: keeps registry cluster nodes agreeing (ZooKeeper, etcd, consul).

Check yourself

During a traffic spike, auto-scaling adds six new instances of the Payment service, but the callers are configured with the two original IP addresses. What happens, and what is the fix?

All traffic still goes to the two original instances.

  • New instances sit idle → the spike is not absorbed
  • Original instance restarts at a new address → calls to it fail
  • Fix: service registry: callers refer to Payment by an alias resolved through it
    • Works like an internal DNS: instances register, callers look up, then connect

The registry must itself be highly available.

A team wants a four-node service-registry cluster 'for extra safety' instead of three. Do you agree?

No.

  • Consensus protocol (Raft or Paxos) needs a majority
  • 4 nodes → need 3 votes → still tolerate only 1 failure, the same as 3 nodes
  • 5 nodes → tolerate 2

The fourth node adds cost and coordination without adding failure tolerance → hence odd sizes such as three or five are the norm.

Services run on a container orchestrator with built-in DNS-based discovery. A teammate wants to add a library-based registry with client and server code as well. Advise.

Use the built-in DNS, not a library-based registry.

  • Built-in DNS already provides registration and lookup by alias, with no discovery code in the services
  • Library-based registry needs client and server code → takes engineers away from business requirements
  • A second registry = another component to keep highly available

Add one only if some services run outside the orchestrator and must be discovered the same way.

Deployment strategies

Four ways to release a new version (blue/green, rolling, canary, shadowing) and when to choose each.

Pick a strategy from business context

Questions to ask:

  • Downtime tolerance → how tolerant are customers of downtime (nines; banks, e-commerce)?
  • Global users? Are users global?
  • Release frequency → how often do you release?
  • Rollback speed → how fast can you roll back?
  • Experiment in production? (A/B tests, passive machine learning models)

Four deployment strategies

  • Blue/green → keeps old and new environments available and switches live traffic
  • Rolling → progressively replaces old instances
  • Canary → a small share of live requests goes to the new version (lecture uses 5–10%)
  • Shadowing → sends copies to the new version but discards its responses

Canary = low-risk, experiment-friendly

  • Only a small set of users or servers sees the release
  • Must be incremental and backward compatible for easy rollback
  • Use it for →
    • cohort behaviour
    • evaluating new ML models
    • unpredictable feature behaviour

Rolling deployments

  • Gradually replace instances while others serve traffic
  • Avoid duplicating a full environment
  • Seamless service needs →
    • sufficient healthy capacity
    • traffic draining
    • compatible versions
  • Temporary extra capacity may still be needed

"No downtime" is a design goal, not an automatic property.

Choose by risk

  • Rolling → compatible routine patches
  • Canary → limited real-user exposure
  • Blue/green → fast traffic switch-back while both environments remain ready
  • Shadowing → compares behaviour without returning new responses; mirrored writes must be isolated to avoid duplicate side effects

None automatically reverses database changes.

Blue/green
Two full environments; switch over once the new one is verified.
Rolling update
Gradual one-by-one replacement of old instances.
Canary release
New version serves a small percentage of traffic first.
Shadowing
Traffic is mirrored to the new version whose responses are discarded.

Check yourself

A bank must release a backward-compatible bug fix with no downtime and has no budget for a second full environment. Which strategy fits?

Rolling update.

  • Progressively replaces V1 instances with V2
  • No downtime or lost requests, no extra resources
  • What the lecture recommends for bug fixes and patches

Needs enough healthy capacity while instances are swapped; V1 and V2 must stay compatible, because both serve traffic during the rollout and rollback.

A new fraud-detection model must be evaluated on real traffic without affecting any customer's decision. Canary or shadowing?

Shadowing, not canary.

  • Shadowing: copies of requests go to V2 while customers get V1's responses → compare decisions without exposing anyone to the new model
  • Canary would let 5 to 10% of customers be judged by an unproven model

Shadowing needs V2's writes and external side effects isolated, because ignoring its response does not stop those from happening.

During a rolling update, V2 starts writing a new required column that V1 does not fill. A rollback to V1 then fails with errors. Which rule was broken?

Broken rule: backward compatibility.

  • Rolling and canary releases run old and new versions side by side and must allow rollback
  • → Each new version must be backward compatible with existing data and callers
  • Fix: make the column optional, or give it a default, until every version that writes the table fills it
  • Breaking changes need incremental releases rather than one step
A major release must be switchable back within seconds if anything looks wrong, and the budget allows double capacity for a short time. Which strategy fits, and what does it not protect against?

Blue/green.

  • V2 runs alongside V1; traffic switches only once V2 is confirmed working → switching back is near-instant
  • Cost: a second full environment for the duration

Does not protect against data V2 has already written to shared stores: switching traffic does not undo it, so the data must stay readable by V1.

Deployment case: splitting the Payment table

A breaking database change must be broken into backward-compatible increments (see the separate 05c case study).

The scenario: splitting Payment

  • Payment microservice stores all payment types in one Payment table
  • Analysis shows Cheque and Apple Pay capture different fields
  • So split the table into ChequePayment and ApplePayPayment
  • Version 1 writes to Payment; Version 2 writes to the two new tables

Why it is hard

  • Old data must be migrated
  • The change is not backward compatible → old payments could not be read

The cure is planning: incremental releases 1.1, 1.2 ... finally 2.0, each choosing a suitable deployment strategy.

Backward compatibility
New version still works with old data and callers, enabling rollback.

Check yourself

V1 of the Payment service writes everything to one Payment table; V2 will write to ChequePayment and ApplePayPayment instead. Why can't this be done as a single rolling update to V2?

The change is not backward compatible.

  • V2 cannot retrieve historical payments still in the Payment table
  • Rolling update: V1 and V2 share traffic → each would miss the other's writes
  • Rolling back would also hide payments V2 had accepted

The lecture breaks it into incremental releases (1.1, 1.2, then 2.0) so each step stays compatible.

Step 1 of the case creates the two new tables with a rolling update and no code change. Why is this step safe, and why do it on its own?
  • Safe: no running code reads or writes the new tables yet → V1 is unaffected
  • Rollback just drops two empty tables
  • On its own: separates the schema expansion (database change) from any behaviour change → if a later step fails, the cause is easier to isolate
  • Later releases can rely on the tables already existing

Circuit breaker

Stop a failing dependency from dragging callers down: fail fast, retry carefully, with the logic in an interceptor rather than business code.

The problem: cascading failure

Payment is down, or so slow that requests time out:

  1. Agency keeps calling Payment
  2. Agency's thread pool clogs
  3. Agency can no longer serve other calls
  4. Requests to Agency clog in turn

Breaker behaviour

  1. Count dependency errors or timeouts in a window
  2. Open when a threshold is exceeded and fail fast
  3. After a reset interval, allow limited probes
  4. Close only on success

A retry policy is separate: use bounded backoff for transient failures without overwhelming recovery.

Implement via interceptors, not code

  • Coding forwarding/blocking logic into Agency is not a business problem
  • It would change per protocol (HTTP, gRPC, Thrift)
  • Libraries or a mesh →
    • intercept all outgoing requests
    • track failures in a time window
    • fail beyond the threshold
    • retry some requests after the window
  • Little or no code
  • Options →
    • Resilience4J
    • Sentinel
    • Hystrix (not active)
    • Istio service mesh (Envoy)

Circuit breaker is a state machine

  • Closed → allow all
  • Open → deny all
  • Half-Open → allow some
Circuit breaker
Interceptor that stops calls to a failing service and probes it again later.
Exponential backoff
Retry delays that double each time (50, 100, 200 ms ...).
Cascading failure
One slow or failed service exhausting others' resources up the call chain.

Check yourself

The Payment service becomes extremely slow, and soon the Agency service stops answering even requests that never touch Payment. Explain why, and how a circuit breaker helps.
  • Why: Agency's request threads all block waiting on Payment → its thread pool clogs → it cannot serve anything
  • Its own callers then clog too → cascading failure
  • Circuit breaker
    • Counts failures and timeouts in a window
    • Past a threshold → opens → fails calls to Payment immediately
    • Agency's threads stay free for other work

Callers get an error or fallback for payment features until Payment recovers.

A team configures its circuit breaker with a reset timeout of zero and lets all traffic through once the breaker is half-open. What goes wrong?
  • Half-open state is meant to let a few sample requests test whether the dependency has recovered after a rest period
  • No timeout + all traffic let through → a recovering Payment service is flooded immediately → probably fails again
  • → The breaker flaps between open and closed
  • Fix: give it a real interval, use exponential backoff, and send only a subset of requests while half-open
Developers plan to hand-code circuit-breaking logic around every outgoing call. Some services use HTTP and others gRPC. What would you suggest?

Use an interceptor instead, through a resilience library or a service mesh.

  • Circuit breaking is not a business problem
  • Hand-written logic would need rewriting for each protocol
  • Interceptor tracks failures, opens the circuit and retries samples with little or no code

Cost: another layer to configure and understand when debugging.

Rate limiting

Protect limited resources and spending by capping traffic per caller, again through interceptors.

Why rate limit

  • Not all users are equal → conditional limits by plan
  • Services have limited CPU, memory and IOPS, and limited budget
  • Unrestricted traffic invites DDoS

How rate limiting works

  1. An interceptor intercepts all requests
  2. Extracts username, source IP, source service or user plan
  3. Consults a fast in-memory quota database to verify and update limits and usage

Little or no code. Options: Resilience4J, Sentinel, Istio, all cloud providers.

Circuit breaker vs rate limiter

  • Circuit breaker → protects the caller from a failing callee
  • Rate limiter → protects the callee from excessive callers

Both limit interaction.

Rate limiting
Capping requests per caller, plan or source, enforced by an interceptor.

Check yourself

On a shared API, one free-plan user's script sends 10,000 requests a minute and slows the service for paying enterprise customers. What would you put in place?

Conditional rate limits enforced by an interceptor.

  • Reads the username, source IP, source service and plan from each request → checks them against a quota store
  • Free-plan users get a lower limit than enterprise users → one script cannot take capacity from everyone
  • Also protects limited CPU, memory and spending
  • Blunts denial-of-service attempts
To avoid running another component, a teammate proposes keeping rate-limit counters in the main relational database. Do you agree?

No.

  • The quota check runs on every request → must add minimal latency
  • Hence the lecture's fast in-memory quota store
  • Counters in the main database → a round trip and write load on every call → could slow the very service you are protecting

The in-memory store must be shared across instances; briefly losing counts on a restart is usually acceptable for quotas.

A healthy service is being overloaded by one customer, while a different dependency is timing out. Which control fits each problem?
  • Overloaded by one customer → rate-limit the excessive customer, protecting the service's capacity for others
  • Dependency timing out → circuit breaker on it → callers stop waiting on it and fail fast
  • Rate limiting controls incoming traffic volume; a circuit breaker reacts to failures of something you call

Using one in place of the other would not help.

Distributed tracing

Follow one user request across many services using a shared ID, so failures are found quickly and MTTR drops.

Problem without tracing

One request touches many services; without tracing:

  • Hard to debug
  • Slow fixes, so MTTR rises
  • Teams lose communication

Tracing mechanism

  • Propagate a trace ID in request headers across services
  • Reuse incoming context; create a new trace at an entry point when needed
  • Instrumentation creates spans and records timing and parent/child relationships

A shared ID correlates work; it does not make the request a single database transaction.

Trace vs span

Lecture presents OpenTracing and a Tracer API.

  • Trace → follows an end-to-end request
  • Span → records one unit of work, such as an HTTP call or database query
  • One service may produce several spans, so service count and span count need not match

Tracing tools

  • Jaeger (CNCF)
  • Spring Sleuth
  • Zipkin
  • Datadog
  • Stackdriver
Trace
End-to-end record of a request or business operation across components; not a database transaction.
Span
Timed unit of work within a trace; one service can create several spans.
MTTR
Mean time to recover.

Check yourself

A request enters through the gateway, which calls Order, which calls Payment. Payment generates a new UUID instead of reusing the incoming trace header. What breaks?

The trace splits in two.

  • You can no longer follow the request from the gateway to Payment
  • A failure in Payment cannot be tied to the user request that caused it
  • Rule: only the root creates the ID; any service that finds the header populated reuses it
  • Tracing libraries enforce this by intercepting requests → safer than hand-written code in each service
Users report intermittent checkout failures spanning five services. The team spent two days logging into each container to find the failing one. What would cut that time?

Distributed tracing + centralised logs.

  • Distributed tracing with a shared trace ID shows the spans for each request → the failing or slow service stands out
  • Centralised logs searched by that ID → the detail without logging into containers
  • Untraceable problems are what make MTTR rise significantly

Cost: instrumenting services and running the tracing and logging back-ends.

In a 4-second checkout trace, the Payment span takes 3.8 seconds and contains a child span 'fraud-check' of 3.7 seconds. Where do you look first, and why does the child span matter?

Look at the fraud check first: almost all of Payment's time is spent there.

  • Span = one unit of work
  • Services can create child spans through the tracer API to break their own work down
  • Without that child span → you would only know Payment was slow, not which part

Distributed transactions and the Saga pattern

Why two-phase commit does not fit microservices, and how sagas use compensating transactions with orchestration or choreography.

Business transactions span services

They need consistency. The traditional answer is two-phase commit (prepare, then commit), but:

  • Not all databases support ACID
  • Platforms use several databases
  • 2PC needs synchronous communication, while modern systems are asynchronous

Skipping transactions is also risky, because partial executions are dangerous.

Saga: local commits + compensation

  • Commit local steps; compensate completed steps after a later failure
  • Lecture example →
    • CreateOrder and CreateMaidOrder precede MakePayment
    • if payment fails: cancel MaidOrder, then Order
  • Compensation is a new business action, not an isolated database rollback
  • Compensation may itself require retry

Saga coordination: two styles

  • Orchestrator → stores progress and directs forward and compensating steps; its availability and contention need attention
  • Choreography → distributes reactions among services; avoids one central controller but makes the complete flow harder to debug

Both need reliable, duplicate-safe processing.

Saga expectations

  • A shared traceId is very important
  • No read isolation → users may read data from incomplete transactions or in-progress cancellations
Saga
Business transaction made of local transactions with compensations, instead of 2PC.
Compensating transaction
Action that semantically undoes an earlier step (CancelPayment).
Two-phase commit
Prepare phase then commit phase; needs synchronous coordination.

Check yourself

Order data lives in a relational database and MaidOrder in a store without distributed-transaction support, and services talk asynchronously. A teammate proposes two-phase commit across them. Do you agree?

No: use a saga.

  • Two-phase commit needs ACID-capable databases and synchronous communication; this platform has neither
  • Saga: each service runs a local transaction
  • Each step Ti has a compensating transaction Ci that undoes it if a later step fails

Trade-off: you give up atomic isolation in exchange for loose coupling and asynchronous calls.

In the Maid2Order saga, CreateOrder and CreateMaidOrder succeed, then MakePayment fails. What runs next, and in what order?

The compensations for the completed steps run in reverse:

  • 1. CancelMaidOrder in MaidOrderManager
  • 2. CancelOrder in CustomerManager
  • Asynchronous like the forward steps → each compensation must be safe to retry and handle duplicate messages
  • A shared trace ID shows whether the whole saga, including compensation, completed
A customer sees a maid order as created, and a minute later it disappears because payment failed. Is this a bug?

Not in itself.

  • Sagas have no read isolation → other users and screens can see data from transactions still in progress or being compensated
  • Expect it and design for it, e.g. show such orders as pending until the saga completes
  • If customers must never see unconfirmed orders → the read path has to filter on saga state
The team must debug failed orders quickly. Should the three-service order saga be orchestrated or choreographed?

Orchestrated.

  • Orchestrator holds the logic and publishes each transaction and compensating event → progress and failure points visible in one place
  • Drawback: can become a single point of failure or contention → must be made highly available
  • Choreography spreads logic across services → harder to debug

Both still need tracing with a shared trace ID.

Metrics

What to measure (infra, application, availability) and the key formulas: uptime, nines, yield and harvest.

Metrics serve business goals

  • Lower cost
  • Faster APIs
  • Better client retention and service
  • Meeting SLAs

'If you can't measure it, you can't improve it.'

Infrastructure metrics

  • CPU and memory
  • Request volume (IOPS)
  • Availability
  • Peak usage time
  • Average response time, with outliers and percentiles
  • Success/failure rate
  • Payload sizes
  • Garbage collection time

Application metrics

  • Task success → did the transaction finish or the customer drop out?
  • Clarity → ease of learning and use (heatmaps)
  • Happiness → returning visitors, visit frequency

Time-based availability

  • Lecture uses → (MTBF − MTTR) / MTBF, treating MTBF as the complete failure-to-failure interval including recovery
  • Worked example → 1000 h between failures, 1 h recovery: 999/1000 = 99.9%
  • If a problem defines MTBF as operating time only → use MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)
  • State the convention; the formulas are not interchangeable definitions
  • Nines → downtime/year
    • three nines ≈ 9 h
    • four nines ≈ 50 min
    • five nines ≈ 5 min

Yield vs harvest

  • Yield = successful requests / total requests → measures service actually received
  • Harvest = available data / total data → measures completeness

A service can stay up yet return errors or incomplete data, so these measures answer different questions.

Metrics tech

  • Prometheus time-series DB with Grafana
  • TICK stack → Telegraf, InfluxDB, Chronograf, Kapacitor
  • Splunk metrics
  • Metrics-as-a-service
Uptime
Fraction of time available; the lecture uses (MTBF − MTTR) / MTBF with the full interval in the denominator.
Yield
Successful requests divided by total requests.
Harvest
Data available divided by total data.

Check yourself

A service fails on average every 500 hours and takes 2 hours to recover. Does it meet a 99.9% uptime target by the lecture's formula, and what would it take to meet it?
  1. Uptime = (MTBF - MTTR) / MTBF = (500 - 2) / 500 = 99.6% → misses 99.9%
  2. To meet it with the same failure rate: MTTR must fall to 0.5 hours, since (500 - 0.5) / 500 = 99.9%

So faster recovery, through tracing, central logs and quick rollback, is as much a lever as failing less often.

An API answers every request successfully but returns only half of the requested dataset because some data servers are down. What do yield and harvest show?
  • Yield = successful requests / total → 100%, if those responses count as successful
  • Harvest = data available / total data → 50%
  • Yield alone would hide the problem → define success according to what users need and report both

Which matters more depends on whether a partial answer is useful to the user.

To show that an API meets 'responses within 3 seconds for 90% of requests', a teammate reports an average response time of 1.2 seconds. Does that show the target is met?

No. An average hides outliers.

  • A mostly fast API can still have well over 10% of requests above 3 seconds
  • Measure the 90th percentile directly over a defined window and load
  • Hence the lecture lists response time with outliers and percentiles
  • Also report the failure rate: failed requests are not fast successes
A checkout API shows 99.99% yield and low latency, yet completed purchases have dropped by 20%. Which metrics would you look at?

Application metrics, not infrastructure metrics.

  • Task success: do transactions end in success, or do customers drop out?
  • Clarity: how easily customers can use the product, e.g. through heatmaps
  • Infrastructure metrics show the API responds, not that users complete what they came for
  • Happiness signals such as returning visitors → whether the drop is lasting

Logging, monitoring and summary

Centralised log aggregation patterns and how all the tools map to platform goals.

Why log

Engineers spend much of their time debugging, analysing and tuning. Typically log:

  • Authentication/authorisation successes and failures
  • Changes to data entities
  • Access to external resources (database, files, queues)
  • Configuration
  • Application errors
  • Feature usage

Centralise logs

With many components, one place to query and analyse beats logging into every machine or container. Three shipping patterns:

  1. Direct → app ships formatted logs directly to the central server
  2. Via transformer → app ships raw logs to a transformation and shipping server that parses them into the central server's format
  3. Agent + parser → app writes to a local file; a lightweight shipping agent sends raw logs to a remote parser; the heavy transformer stores into the central server

Logging tech

  • ELK (Elasticsearch, Logstash, Kibana) with FluentD or Filebeat
  • Splunk
  • AWS centralised logging
  • Stackdriver
  • Papertrail
  • Loggly

Summary mapping: business intent → tool

  • Seamless interaction → deployment strategies (rollout, canary)
  • Scaling → auto-scaling
  • Limiting → circuit breaker, rate limiting, retries
  • Experimentation/traffic management → canary
  • Data-driven intelligence → logs and metrics (observability)
  • Faster issue resolution → logging, monitoring and distributed tracing

Security (mutual TLS, reducing attack surface) is mentioned but not covered.

Observability
Understanding system behaviour from collected logs, metrics and traces.
Log shipping agent
Lightweight process sending local log files to a remote parser (e.g. Filebeat).

Check yourself

Applications parse and format their logs before shipping them, and CPU on the application hosts has risen by 15%. What pattern would reduce this?

Ship raw logs, parse elsewhere → parsing moves off the application hosts.

  1. Write logs to a local file
  2. A lightweight shipping agent sends raw logs to a separate, heavier parser and transformer
  3. It stores them in the central log server

Cost: another tier to run, and some delay before logs are searchable.

After an incident, the team cannot tell who changed a customer's bank details or when. What should have been logged, and where?

What to log, all shipped to a central log store:

  • Authentication and authorisation successes and failures
  • Changes to data entities
  • Access to external resources such as databases and queues
  • Why centralise: the change may have passed through several services, and searching each container is slow
  • Trace ID in each entry → the whole change can be followed across services
In one incident, a partner floods the API, a downstream service fails slowly, and nobody can find where the errors start. Map each problem to the toolbox.
  • Partner flood → rate limiting
  • Slow downstream service → contain the slow dependency with a circuit breaker, timeouts and careful retries, which also limit the interaction
  • Can't find where errors start → distributed tracing plus centralised logging and monitoring, which lower reaction time

Each tool addresses a different failure, so having one does not replace the others.

A platform runs ten microservices on a container orchestrator, each auto-scaling from 2 to 20 instances. Design how services find each other, how that mechanism stays available, and what you would not build yourself.
  1. Finding each other: callers refer to each service by alias, resolved through a registry
    • Works like an internal DNS: instances register, callers look up, then connect
    • → Scaling and restarts never require configuration changes
  2. Staying available: a consensus-backed cluster of three or five nodes
    • Tolerates one or two failures respectively
    • An even size adds nothing
  3. Don't build it yourself: use the orchestrator's built-in DNS discovery
    • Writing client and server discovery code takes engineers away from business requirements

Discovery handles traffic between services; user traffic still enters through the gateway.

The Payment service becomes slow, and the Agency service, which calls it, starts failing all its requests. Identify the cause, propose a countermeasure, and give the countermeasure's main drawback.
  1. Cause: a cascading failure
    • Agency's threads block waiting on Payment → its thread pool clogs → it cannot serve even unrelated requests
    • Its callers clog in turn
  2. Countermeasure: a circuit breaker in an interceptor, through a resilience library or service mesh
    • Counts failures and timeouts in a window
    • Opens to fail fast
    • Goes half-open after an interval to send sample requests, with exponential backoff
    • Closes on success
    • Add rate limiting if Payment is overloaded by volume
  3. Drawback
    • While the circuit is open, payment-dependent features fail fast or degrade for every user
    • Badly tuned thresholds can open the circuit on brief blips
Choose a deployment strategy for (a) a routine, backward-compatible security patch; (b) a new recommendation model whose effect on user behaviour must be measured; (c) a major release that must be switchable back instantly. Justify each and give one shared precondition.
  1. (a) Security patch → rolling update
    • No downtime and no extra resources, ideal for patches
    • Provided capacity holds during replacement
  2. (b) Recommendation model → canary with 5 to 10% of traffic
    • Assesses cohort behaviour live
    • Shadowing would let you compare outputs without exposure but cannot measure user reaction
  3. (c) Major release → blue/green
    • V2 runs alongside V1 and traffic switches back at once
    • Cost: double capacity

Shared precondition: versions must stay backward compatible with the data, because switching traffic does not undo writes.

An order spans CustomerManager (CreateOrder), MaidOrderManager (CreateMaidOrder) and PaymentManager (MakePayment), each with its own database and asynchronous messaging. Design the consistency mechanism, its failure handling, and what users may observe.

Use a saga, because two-phase commit needs synchronous calls and ACID support across all databases.

  • Failure handling: each step has a compensation; if MakePayment fails → run CancelMaidOrder then CancelOrder
  • Coordination
    • Orchestrator for central visibility, made highly available to avoid a single point of failure
    • or choreography for independent reactions, at the cost of harder debugging
  • Trace every step with a shared trace ID; make steps and compensations idempotent
  • Users may see in-progress or cancelled orders, because sagas have no read isolation → show such orders as pending
Over a 90-day quarter (2,160 hours), a platform had four failures, each taking 3 hours to recover. The SLA promises 99.9%. Using the lecture's metrics, does it comply, which metric matters most to users, and what would you change?
  1. Comply? No
    • MTBF = 2,160 / 4 = 540 hours
    • Uptime = (540 - 3) / 540 = 99.44% → misses 99.9% (about 9 hours of downtime a year)
  2. Matters most to users: yield, more than uptime
    • An outage at 3 a.m. with no traffic costs little, while one at peak costs many requests
    • So also report successful over total requests and the harvest of data served
  3. What to change
    • To comply with the same failure rate → MTTR must fall to about 0.54 hours → invest in tracing, centralised logs and fast rollback
    • Reduce failures with canaries and circuit breakers
Users report intermittent errors on a flow that crosses five services, and nobody can reproduce them. Which observability tools would you use, in what order, and what does each contribute?
  1. Metrics first
    • Error rate and latency percentiles per service → show when the errors happen and which service's numbers move
  2. Distributed tracing
    • One ID created at the root and propagated in headers
    • Shows which span fails or slows in an affected request
    • Child spans narrow it down within a service
  3. Centralised logs last
    • Searched by that trace ID → the exception and context without logging into each container

Together they lower MTTR; cost: instrumenting every service and running the back-ends.
