Reusable Services and API Gateway

79 slides · about 37 min · open the deck · JSON

Reusable services need explicit boundaries and deliberate integration. Context mappings describe relationships between bounded contexts; aggregates protect local invariants; domain events carry changes to other aggregates or services. This topic applies those ideas to car rental, compares orchestration with choreography, then explains gateway discovery and client SDKs.

Big ideas

  • A bounded context is the unit of a reusable service; each has its own ubiquitous language, so integrations must mediate between languages.
  • Context mapping kinds: Shared Kernel, Partnership, Customer-Supplier, Conformist, Anticorruption Layer, Open Host Service, Published Language, Separate Ways.
  • Avoid shared internal databases and files across contexts. Messaging removes temporal coupling; synchronous REST or RPC suits interactions requiring an immediate reply.
  • An aggregate is a transactional consistency boundary with one root: modify one aggregate per transaction, keep it small, reference others by identity only.
  • Domain events record business outcomes in past tense. Persist the aggregate change and event together, then deliver to subscribers that update in separate transactions.
  • Messaging needs at-least-once delivery plus idempotent receivers.
  • Orchestration = central brain, explicit, god-service risk; choreography = event-driven, implicit, loosely coupled, needs monitoring.
  • An API gateway is the server-side discovery router: it load balances, offers client-specific APIs, aggregates calls and translates protocols.

Characteristics of microservices

The defining traits of the microservice style (Fowler) and how each contrasts with the monolith or ESB way of working.

  • Microservices are small services, each in its own process, built around business capabilities and independently deployable. They talk through lightweight mechanisms (often an HTTP resource API), with minimal central management and freedom in language and data store.
  • Componentization via services, not shared libraries. Services are out-of-process, so they deploy independently, have explicit interfaces and keep encapsulation. The cost: remote calls are slower than in-process calls so APIs must be coarser-grained, and moving responsibility between components across process boundaries is harder.
  • Organise around business capabilities (Conway's law). A system's structure copies the organisation's communication structure, so use cross-functional teams (UX, database, project management) per service. Siloed functional teams turn simple changes into cross-team projects and tempt people to push logic into whatever application they can reach.
  • Smart endpoints, dumb pipes vs ESB. Microservices put the logic in the endpoints and use a simple message router or REST, which is decoupled, cohesive and cache-friendly. An ESB puts routing, transformation, choreography and business rules into the bus, using heavy protocols such as BPEL or WS-Choreography.
  • Decentralised governance and data: cross-functional teams own services and their data, may choose different stacks, and operate what they build. Local transactions protect each service; cross-service work often uses eventual consistency and compensation. Independent evolution is a benefit, balanced against distributed-system overhead.
  • Automation and design for failure. CI/CD pipelines with automated tests, deployment and production management are the norm. Any service call can fail, so clients degrade gracefully and teams monitor both architecture metrics (database throughput) and business metrics (orders per minute).
Conway's law
A system's design mirrors the communication structure of the organisation that builds it.
Smart endpoints, dumb pipes
Logic lives in services; the messaging layer only routes.
Compensating operation
An action that undoes or corrects an earlier step when there is no distributed transaction.

Check yourself

A team splits a monolith by turning each in-process method call into a remote call one for one. A single page now makes 40 remote calls and is slow. Diagnose the problem.
Remote calls cost far more than in-process calls, so remote APIs need to be coarser-grained than the library-style interfaces they replaced. The services should expose fewer, larger operations around business capabilities, which may mean revisiting the boundaries. Moving responsibilities between services later is harder once they sit across process boundaries, so the split deserves care up front.
Order and Inventory now own separate databases. A manager asks for 'one ACID transaction across both, like before'. How do you respond?
Microservices favour decentralised data, where each service manages its own database and coordinates with others without a shared transaction. That means eventual consistency plus compensating operations, such as releasing stock if the order fails. If the business truly needs both changes to commit atomically, that suggests the data belongs in one service rather than a distributed transaction.
A company has separate UI, backend and database teams, and every new feature becomes a cross-team project needing budget approval. What does Conway's law predict about its system, and what would you change?
The system will mirror those silos: layers owned by different teams, tangled together, with logic pushed into whichever part a team can touch. Organise cross-functional teams around business capabilities, each with the full range of skills from user experience to database, so each team's service matches a capability. Teams then own their service end to end, including running it.
An architect proposes putting routing, message transformation and business rules into a central message bus so the services can stay simple. Critique the proposal.
That is the enterprise-service-bus style, with smarts in the pipe. Microservices prefer smart endpoints and dumb pipes: the logic stays in cohesive services and the bus only routes messages. A smart central bus couples every service to one shared component and its team, so changes queue up there. The cost of the microservice style is that each service owns more of its own logic.

Decoupling services with context mappings

Bounded contexts (the reusable services) still depend on each other; context mappings are the DDD vocabulary for designing those couplings and the team relationships behind them.

  • Bounded contexts are not fully independent. Each bounded context (BC) has its own ubiquitous language (UL) and domain model, yet one BC may use an entity owned by another, or need to know about events on another's entities. These couplings must be designed deliberately so BCs are not needlessly tied together.
  • A context mapping designs a relationship between two BCs. In upstream/downstream patterns, one supplies a model or service and the other consumes it. Shared Kernel and Partnership instead involve mutual agreement. Different ubiquitous languages still need deliberate mediation; for example Agile Project Management consumes Discussion from Collaboration.
  • Mappings range from tight to loose collaboration. Shared Kernel (small common model, needs constant agreement) and Partnership (teams continuously align schedules) are the closest. Customer-Supplier (supplier upstream, has the final say but tries to serve the customer) and Conformist (downstream just adopts the upstream model) are the upstream/downstream relationships.
  • Conformist vs Anticorruption Layer is the key choice for a downstream team. Conformist fits when the upstream model is large, well established and unresponsive, and translating is too costly. An Anticorruption Layer translates the upstream UL into the downstream UL to protect the downstream model, but costs effort to maintain; it is a great way to integrate with an upstream Big Ball of Mud (legacy).
  • Open Host Service and Published Language make the upstream easy to consume. OHS exposes the context as a set of services via a protocol or interface, reducing the need for an ACL downstream. Published Language is a well-documented exchange format (XML Schema, JSON Schema), and an OHS often provides one. Separate Ways means no integration at all when the payoff is too small.
Bounded context (BC)
A boundary within which one domain model and one ubiquitous language apply; the unit used to find reusable services.
Ubiquitous language (UL)
The shared vocabulary of a BC's team and its model; the same word may mean different things in another BC.
Upstream / Downstream
The supplier of a model or service (U) and its consumer (D).
Anticorruption layer
Downstream translation layer isolating its model from the upstream model.
Open Host Service
Upstream publishes an open protocol/interface to its context.

Check yourself

Your small Shipping team must integrate with a large, established ERP whose team will not change anything for you. Should Shipping be a Conformist or build an Anticorruption Layer?
It depends on whether Shipping's model can live with the ERP's. If it can, and the team cannot sustain the effort of translating, conform: it is cheaper. If the ERP is a Big Ball of Mud that would distort Shipping's model, build an Anticorruption Layer that translates to Shipping's ubiquitous language, accepting the cost of maintaining the translation.
Eight downstream contexts each maintain their own translation of the Customer context's model, and every Customer change breaks several of them. What should the Customer team offer instead?
An Open Host Service, a protocol or set of services giving access to the context, with a Published Language such as a documented JSON Schema. Downstream teams can then consume and translate easily, and an anticorruption layer becomes less crucial for them. The Customer team takes on keeping that public interface stable and versioned.
Marketing wants a small piece of Billing's data, but Billing's model does not really provide what Marketing needs, and integrating would take months. What mapping fits?
Separate Ways: integration would not give a significant payoff because the functionality sought is not fully provided by the source ubiquitous language. Marketing builds its own specialised solution. The cost is some duplicated capability, which is cheaper than months of integration.
Two teams propose a shared kernel of 40 classes so they can stop translating between their contexts. What concerns would you raise?
A shared kernel should be a small common model, and even then it is hard to conceive and maintain, because it needs open communication and constant agreement between both teams. Forty classes would bind their release schedules together. A Customer-Supplier relationship, or an Open Host Service with a Published Language, keeps the teams more independent.

Integration styles between contexts

Choose immediate synchronous replies or asynchronous messages according to the business flow. Reliable messages still require duplicate-safe handling.

  • Avoid integrating through a shared database or file system. The typical styles are RPC via SOAP, RESTful HTTP and messaging.
  • RPC (SOAP) is simple but brittle. It looks like a local call, but the request must cross the network, reach the remote system and return. It is vulnerable to network failure, latency and upstream failure, and strongly couples client to service and to a specific protocol. Best practice: upstream provides an Open Host Service with a Published Language; downstream uses an Anticorruption Layer.
  • RESTful HTTP centres on resources and the four primary operations (POST, GET, PUT, DELETE), giving well-defined APIs. It is still susceptible to network and upstream failure, but mitigates this by relying on the reliability and scalability of the Web.
  • Messaging removes temporal coupling: a publisher can leave a durable event for a consumer to process later. This tolerates temporary consumer outages but accepts delay and requires operating the messaging infrastructure. Use synchronous calls when an immediate result is necessary; neither style removes network failure.
  • Safe delivery and handling: at-least-once delivery retries until acknowledgement, assuming the broker retains the message and recovery is possible. Duplicates can occur, so receivers must de-duplicate or safely reapply changes. Acknowledge after successful processing so failure does not silently discard work.
Temporal coupling
Both parties must be up at the same time, as in blocking calls.
At-least-once delivery
Messages are redelivered until acknowledged, so duplicates are possible.
Idempotent receiver
A receiver whose handling produces the same result however many times a message arrives.

Check yourself

When an order is placed, the Loyalty context must add points. Loyalty is offline for 20 minutes every night for maintenance. Would you integrate with REST or messaging?
Messaging. Order publishes a domain event and Loyalty processes it when it comes back, which removes the temporal coupling of a blocking REST call that would fail or stall orders during the outage. It needs at-least-once delivery and an idempotent receiver in Loyalty. The trade-off is that points appear later rather than immediately.
After a broker redelivery, the Loyalty subscriber processes the same OrderPlaced event twice and awards double points. What is the fix?
At-least-once delivery means redelivery is expected, so the receiver must be idempotent. Record processed event IDs and skip duplicates, or make the update safe to reapply, such as 'set points for order X' instead of 'add points'. Switching off redelivery would just swap duplicates for lost messages.
To save time, the Reporting context reads the Order context's tables directly. What is wrong with this?
Database integration is the type to avoid. It couples Reporting to Order's internal storage, breaks the bounded context's encapsulation, and means any change to Order's schema can break Reporting without warning. Use Order's published API, or subscribe to its domain events and build Reporting's own read model, at the cost of some lag.
A team chose SOAP RPC between two contexts because the call looks just like a local procedure call. What risks does that hide, and what practices reduce them?
The call still crosses the network, so it lacks robustness against latency, network failure and upstream failure, and it couples the client strongly to the service and protocol. The lecture's practices are an Open Host Service with a Published Language upstream and an Anticorruption Layer downstream. If the flow does not need an immediate answer, messaging would remove the temporal coupling.

Aggregates

An aggregate is a cluster of entities inside one BC that is loaded, changed and saved as a single unit; the rules of thumb say how to size them, reference them and keep them consistent.

  • An aggregate is a transactional consistency boundary. It is a cluster of domain entities in one BC treated as a unit (think of elements that live and die together), with one entity as the Aggregate Root that owns all others, plus optional Value Objects. In one transaction modify and commit only one aggregate; consistency across aggregates is handled by separate transactions.
  • Entity vs Value Object vs Aggregate Root. An Entity has a unique identity and is usually mutable (state changes over time). A Value Object has no identity, is an immutable conceptual whole with attributes and behaviour, and qualifies (describes, quantifies, measures) an entity. The Aggregate Root is the root entity that owns everything else.
  • Protect business invariants inside the boundary. Example: when all Task instances have hoursRemaining of zero, the BacklogItem status must be DONE; that must hold at the end of the transaction. Business logic belongs in the aggregate, so avoid an Anemic Domain Model (only getters/setters) and do not leak logic into application services or helper classes.
  • Design small aggregates, single-minded (Single Responsibility). Small ones load quickly, use less memory, collect faster, succeed in transactions more often and are easier to work on and test. For example, break a huge Product (containing many BacklogItems, Releases, Sprints) into separate aggregates.
  • Reference other aggregates by identity only. BacklogItem, Release and Sprint hold a ProductId, not a Product object. Identities are easy to persist and the same rule applies across BCs; model identities as value objects (immutable).
  • Eventual consistency between aggregates: BacklogItem stores SprintId and records BacklogItemCommitted in one transaction; Sprint later stores BacklogItemId in its own. They may be in the same BC or different BCs. If one business invariant requires an atomic update, reconsider the boundary: consider merging within a BC, or revisit the cross-context business process.
  • Persistence: one repository per aggregate. The repository persists the whole aggregate (root, entities, value objects), possibly via several DAOs and tables; a Factory Method may build complex aggregates. An aggregate (domain model) is not a DTO; one aggregate may map to several DTOs.
Aggregate
Cluster of entities and value objects in a BC treated as one unit of consistency and transaction.
Aggregate root
The one entity through which the aggregate is accessed; owns the other elements.
Value object
Immutable, identity-less object that describes or measures an entity.
Business invariant
A business rule that must be true at the end of every transaction.
Anemic domain model
Anti-pattern: aggregates with accessors only, logic elsewhere.

Check yourself

A Sprint aggregate holds direct object references to all its BacklogItems, which hold all their Tasks. Loading a sprint takes three seconds and concurrent edits often fail. What would you change?
Design small aggregates and reference other aggregates by identity. Make BacklogItem its own aggregate that holds a SprintId value object, and have Sprint hold BacklogItemIds rather than objects. Smaller aggregates load faster, use less memory and succeed more often under concurrency. Changes that span both are then made through domain events in separate transactions, which means eventual consistency.
The business says that when a backlog item is committed, the sprint's capacity must never be exceeded, checked at the same commit. Today Sprint and BacklogItem are separate aggregates updated by a domain event. What do you do?
This invariant must hold within one transaction, and the delay of a domain event would allow unacceptable inconsistency. So consider merging the commitment data into one aggregate within the same bounded context, which the lecture advises when two must be updated immediately one after the other. The cost is a larger aggregate with more contention, so first check whether the business could accept detecting and correcting an over-commit instead.
In code review, the Order aggregate has only getters and setters, while an OrderService checks that the total does not exceed the credit limit and then sets the status. What is the problem?
It is an anaemic domain model: business logic has leaked out of the aggregate into an application service. Other code paths can set the status without checking the rule, so the invariant is not protected inside the boundary. Move the rule into a method on Order, such as place(), that enforces it before changing state.
A team models Money as an entity with its own ID and table, and Customer as a value object. Critique the model.
It is backwards. Money is a value object: it has no identity of its own, is immutable, and describes an amount. Customer is an entity with a unique identity whose attributes change over time. Making Money an entity adds needless IDs and invites shared mutable amounts, while making Customer a value object makes it impossible to track one customer across changes.

Domain events

A domain event records a business-significant occurrence in a BC; this section covers what they are, why order matters, and how to identify, name, shape and commit them.

  • A domain event (DE) is a record of a business-significant occurrence in a BC. It is the loosely coupled integration mechanism: the means for one aggregate or BC to tell others what happened, used for messaging-type context mappings and for propagating change between aggregates.
  • Order matters for causal consistency. Causally related operations must be seen in the same order by every dependent node of a distributed system. In the wallet example, Sue's 'I lost my wallet!' must be followed by Gary's 'That's terrible!'; if replies and posts are seen out of order, 'That's great!' appears after the lost-wallet post and makes no sense.
  • Identify events from the integration points. For each context mapping of messaging type, find the business functions that rely on it and the events they require; also add an event for each propagation of change from one aggregate to another.
  • Name events as past-tense verbs. They are things that already happened. If a command triggered the event, name the event after the command: CreateProduct leads to ProductCreated; CommitBacklogItemToSprint leads to BacklogItemCommitted.
  • Event properties: in the lecture, copy the business properties of the triggering command, then enrich where needed. ProductCreated might carry tenantId, productId, name and description. Include enough context for subscribers to react without querying the publisher; event data describes the completed outcome.
  • Commit state and event together: changing BacklogItem and durably recording BacklogItemCommitted must succeed or fail in the same transaction. Recording an event is distinct from delivering it over a network: after commit, deliver it reliably to subscribers, which update their own aggregates in later transactions.
Domain event
Immutable record of a business-significant thing that happened in a BC, named in past tense.
Causal consistency
Causally related events are observed in the same order by all dependent nodes.
Enriched event
An event carrying extra data so consumers need not call back the publisher.

Check yourself

A service commits an Order and then publishes OrderPlaced as a separate step. Occasionally the process crashes between the two. What is the consequence, and what is the fix?
The order exists but the event is lost, so subscribers such as Inventory and Billing never learn about it and stay stale. Commit the modified aggregate and the domain event in the same transaction, then deliver recorded events reliably afterwards, including after recovery. Subscribers still need to be idempotent, because that delivery can repeat.
Shipping receives OrderPlaced events carrying only the orderId and calls the Order API back for the address and tenantId on every event. The Order API is now overloaded. What would you change?
Make the event carry all the properties of the command that caused it, and enrich it with extra data such as tenantId and the delivery address, so consumers need not query the publisher. That removes the remote calls and decouples Shipping from Order's availability. The cost is larger events and an event schema that must be kept stable for every consumer.
On a forum, replies sometimes appear on other users' screens before the post they answer. Which property is violated, and what must the design guarantee?
Causal consistency: causally related operations must be seen in the same order by every dependent node. A reply depends on its post, so its event must never be applied before the post's event. Give events an order within each conversation and have consumers apply them in that order, waiting for missing predecessors. Ordering every event globally would be stricter and more costly than needed.
Maid2Order's MaidOrderManager must tell BillingManager through messaging when a customer's usage should be billed. Identify, name and shape the domain event.
Start from the messaging context mapping and the business function that relies on it, billing a customer's maid usage, and ask what occurrence BillingManager needs to know about. Name it in the past tense, for example MaidUsageBillIssued, following the command that caused it. Give it the command's properties, such as the bill, customer and usage IDs, amount and period, and enrich it with anything Billing would otherwise query back for. A name like IssueBill would describe a request, not something that happened.

Car rental example and workshop

A worked case showing how splitting an oversized aggregate and replacing a direct cross-context update with a domain event yields independent microservices.

  • Starting problem: in the Car Rental System, Rental Manager and Vehicle Manager are bounded contexts. Extending a rental needs to verify the vehicle's usability (a call into Vehicle Manager) and update the rental date, but the rental model is assumed to have an excessive memory footprint.
  • Fix: split into smaller aggregates and use an event. Rental Manager is divided into Rent Ctrl and Rent Ext aggregates, Vehicle Manager has Vehicle Ctrl. Rent Ext verifies usability with Vehicle Ctrl, then publishes a Rental Extended event, and Rent Ctrl is updated from that event rather than in the same operation. This applies small aggregates plus eventual consistency.
  • Resulting architecture: each BC becomes a microservice (RentalMgr, VehicleMgr, CustomerMgr) with its own transaction scope, data repository, database, team and code repository. RentalMgr publishes VehicleRented and VehicleReturned domain events. Aggregates and events thus define service boundaries and data ownership.
  • Workshop task: choose two significant services (BCs) from the earlier entity/BC/UL workshop, design their aggregates with roots, entities and value objects, and design at least three domain events with names and assumed properties.

Check yourself

In the car-rental example, the Rental Manager's aggregate was too large, and extending a rental updated the rental record directly. What did the redesign do, and what does it cost?
It split the aggregate into smaller ones (Rent Ctrl and Rent Ext) and replaced the direct update with a Rental Extended domain event, so each transaction changes one small aggregate. That improves memory use, speed and transactional success. The cost is eventual consistency: for a short time the rental date is not yet updated.
After that redesign, a teammate proposes one shared database for RentalMgr, VehicleMgr and CustomerMgr to make reporting easier. How do you respond?
No. In the resulting architecture each service has its own transaction scope, data repository, database, team and code repository, and VehicleRented and VehicleReturned events are how they integrate. A shared database would recouple them through schema changes. Build reporting from those events into its own read model instead, accepting some lag.
For the workshop, design the aggregates of Maid2Order's MaidOrderManager. Which entity is a root, and what do the others reference?
One defensible design makes MaidUsage the aggregate root for a customer's ordered service, holding the CustomerId and a MaidUsagePlanId by identity, since plans are a shared catalogue that changes independently. MaidUsageBill is its own aggregate referencing the MaidUsage by ID, so issuing a bill does not lock the usage. Changes propagate through events such as MaidUsageCreated and MaidUsageBillIssued. If a plan change must be reflected in the bill at the same commit, that is the signal to merge.

Orchestration vs choreography

Two ways to run a business flow that spans several services, with their trade-offs; they can be mixed.

  • Why it arises: as business logic grows, a business function stretches across service boundaries, and the flow must be implemented either by orchestration or choreography (or a mix).
  • Orchestration: a central brain tells others what to do. In the new-customer example, the Customer service creates the record, then requests the Loyalty Points Bank to create a loyalty record, the Post service to dispatch a welcome pack and the Email service to send a welcome email. It is simpler, the flow is explicit and a rules engine can be used, but it tends to create overly smart 'god' services.
  • Choreography: services react to events. The Customer service creates the record and emits an event; Loyalty, Post and Email each receive it and do their part. It is more complex with an implicit flow, relies on domain events and is more loosely coupled, but you must monitor and track that the flow is carried out as expected.
  • Decision rule: prefer orchestration for simplicity and visibility of the flow; prefer choreography for loose coupling and to avoid a god service, accepting the monitoring burden.
Orchestration
A central service directs other services with explicit requests.
Choreography
Services react independently to published events; no central controller.

Check yourself

A new-customer flow currently creates the customer, then loyalty points, a welcome pack and a welcome email. Next quarter three other teams will add SMS, CRM and fraud-screening reactions. Orchestration or choreography?
Choreography. The Customer service emits one event, and each new team subscribes without changing the Customer service, which keeps services loosely coupled. Orchestration would make Customer a growing 'god' service that knows every step. The cost is an implicit flow, so you must monitor that each reaction happens.
The onboarding flow is choreographed, and the operations team cannot tell whether a given customer ever received the welcome pack. What is missing?
Choreography needs monitoring and tracking to confirm that the implicit flow completes as expected. Correlate the events for each customer with a shared ID and trace them, and alert when an expected reaction has not happened within a time limit. If visibility of progress is essential, orchestrate that part, since the lecture says the two can be mixed.
A refund process has strictly ordered steps, and support staff need to see exactly which step each refund has reached. Which approach fits, and what is its risk?
Orchestration: an explicit flow in one coordinator gives a clear view of each refund's progress and enforces the order of steps, and it can use a rules engine. Its risk is an overly smart 'god' service. Keep the coordinator limited to directing the flow, and leave each step's business logic in the service that owns it.

Service discovery and API gateway

What a microservice system needs before it is production ready: service discovery and load balancing, with the API gateway as the single router in front.

  • From sample to production: hard-coded locations miss new or moved instances. Add discovery and load balancing; the sample’s API gateway gives clients one stable entry point. Replicate each service’s owned database for its scaled instances and keep the message broker available. Do not turn this into a database shared across different BCs.
  • Server-side discovery with a service registry. Instances in virtualised or containerised environments change in number and location dynamically, so clients cannot know addresses. The client calls a router (load balancer) at a well-known location; the router queries the service registry, load balances and forwards to an available instance. Instances register themselves with the registry.
  • The API gateway is that router, plus more. It makes clients oblivious to how the application is partitioned, gives the optimal API for each client, lets a client reach multiple services in a single trip, and translates the public API protocol to whatever internal protocols are used.
  • Examples: the production-ready sample uses Spring Cloud tools on Spring Boot (registration and discovery, routing, service-to-service calls, load balancing, circuit breakers, distributed messaging), some originating from Netflix. AWS API Gateway creates, publishes, maintains, monitors and secures APIs at any scale, handling concurrent calls, traffic management, authorisation and access control, monitoring and API version management.
Service registry
Database of available service instances and their locations; instances register themselves.
Server-side discovery
A router queries the registry and forwards requests, so clients need no discovery logic.
API gateway
Single entry point that routes, load balances, adapts APIs per client and translates protocols.

Check yourself

The Gamification service is configured with the Multiplication service's IP address. Operations scale Multiplication to three instances, and later one instance restarts at a new address. What happens?
Gamification keeps calling the one configured address, so the new instances get no traffic, and the call breaks once that instance moves. In virtualised or containerised environments instances are ephemeral, so services need a registry and a router that looks up available instances and load-balances. With server-side discovery the client calls a well-known router and never needs instance addresses.
A web app and a smartwatch app need differently shaped data from the same four services. What can an API gateway do for them, and what does it cost?
The gateway can offer each client its optimal API, aggregate the four calls into one trip, translate the public protocol to internal ones, and hide how the application is partitioned. Clients get simpler and faster, and services can be split or moved behind it. The cost is client-specific gateway logic to maintain, and the gateway becomes a critical component that must be highly available.
After adding a gateway and a registry, the team scales each service to three instances. Which single points of failure remain in the lecture's sample architecture?
The databases shared by each service's instances and the message broker both need high availability. The gateway and registry themselves must also be replicated, or they become new single points of failure. Scaling the stateless service instances alone does not remove these.

Client SDKs and summary

Client libraries that make consuming services convenient, and the wrap-up of how the pieces of the topic fit.

  • SDKs make services easy to consume. AWS provides SDKs for many languages and platforms (Java, .NET, Node.js, Python and others, plus Android, iOS and mobile web), so applications call AWS services through native code.
  • Feign in the lecture’s Spring Cloud stack: declarative clients map interface methods to REST requests and integrate with Eureka, Ribbon and Hystrix for discovery and resilience. This is the illustrated stack, not a requirement for every SDK. Calls still cross a network and can time out or fail.
  • How the pieces fit: context mappings describe dependencies between BCs; aggregates define local transaction scope and reference other aggregates by ID; domain events propagate outcomes. Orchestration or choreography coordinates larger flows. Gateways simplify client access, while SDKs ease calls without removing network boundaries.
Feign
Declarative REST client mapping interface methods to remote HTTP calls; network failure still needs handling.

Check yourself

A Feign client method looks just like a local method call. Can the calling code treat it as equally reliable?
No. It still performs a remote HTTP call, with latency, timeouts and possible partial failure. Discovery and load balancing find a healthy instance, but timeouts, circuit breakers and safe retries are still needed. The convenience hides the network; it does not remove it.
A teammate says: 'Our services follow bounded contexts, so they are decoupled; we don't need context maps, aggregates or domain events.' How do you respond?
Bounded contexts still use each other's entities and need to know about each other's events, so they are not totally decoupled. Context mappings model how they integrate, aggregates break direct references between services and set transaction scope, and domain events give loosely coupled integration. Without these, the coupling still exists, just undesigned and hidden in ad hoc calls and shared data.

Exam-style questions

Answer aloud or on paper before opening.

In an online-auction platform, the Bidding context's Auction aggregate holds every Bid placed on it, often thousands. A live leaderboard is computed by querying the Bidding database each time one of up to 50,000 concurrent viewers refreshes. You doubt viewers will see the latest bids promptly. Identify the most likely cause, propose a countermeasure by revising the domain model, and give its drawback.
Cause: the oversized Auction aggregate makes every bid load and lock a large object graph, so bids contend and transactions slow down, and the leaderboard reads compete with bid writes on the same transactional store. Countermeasure: make Bid its own small aggregate referencing AuctionId, with Auction keeping only the invariants it must protect at commit, such as the current highest valid bid. Publish an enriched BidPlaced event in the same transaction as the bid, and let a Leaderboard context maintain its own read model from those events and push updates to viewers. Drawback: the leaderboard is eventually consistent, events must be applied in order per auction and processed idempotently, and there are more components to run and monitor.
A platform has been split into Order, Inventory and Billing services, and Billing must also integrate with a legacy finance system that nobody will change. Design the context mappings and integration types, and justify each.
Order depends on Inventory as a Customer-Supplier: Inventory, upstream, tries to meet Order's requests but has the final say. Inventory and Billing expose Open Host Services with a Published Language, such as JSON Schema, so downstream contexts can consume them with little translation. Billing protects its model from the legacy finance system with an Anticorruption Layer, accepting the cost of maintaining the translation. Avoid database or file integration. Use messaging with domain events where an immediate reply is not needed, with at-least-once delivery and idempotent receivers, and REST only where the caller needs an answer now.
A customer places an order that must reduce stock held in an Inventory aggregate in another service. Design this with aggregates and domain events, and say what you would do if the business cannot accept delayed stock allocation.
Order commits its change and an OrderPlaced event in one local transaction, referencing products by ID. The event, carrying the order lines and any data Inventory would otherwise query, is delivered reliably, and Inventory decrements stock in its own transaction, handling duplicates idempotently. A failure publishes a compensating outcome, such as StockReservationFailed, so the order can be cancelled. If allocation must be immediate, events cannot promise that: either move the reservation into the same aggregate and context, or reserve stock synchronously before confirming the order, accepting tighter coupling.
A new-customer flow must create loyalty points, dispatch a welcome pack and send an email; legal now requires proof that every customer received the privacy email within 24 hours. Compare orchestration and choreography for this flow and recommend a design.
Orchestration gives an explicit flow in the Customer service, which is simple and easy to audit, but it risks a 'god' service coupled to every step. Choreography has the Customer service emit one event that each service reacts to, which is loosely coupled and easy to extend, but the flow is implicit and must be monitored. A mixed design fits: choreograph the loyalty points and welcome pack, and orchestrate or explicitly track the privacy email, recording its completion against the customer ID and alerting if it is missing after 24 hours. The cost is two styles to understand and operate.
At peak, 500 payments a minute are confirmed. Each confirmation must update the Order context and trigger a customer notification; the Notification service is available 99% of the time. Would you integrate by REST or messaging, and what must be true for your choice to be safe?
Messaging. Publishing PaymentConfirmed lets Payment finish even when Notification is among its 1% of downtime, and the queued events catch up later; synchronous REST would make payment confirmation depend on Notification being up. For safety, the event must be committed with the payment state, delivered at least once, and handled by idempotent receivers in Order and Notification. The cost is that the order status and notification lag slightly behind the payment, so if the user's screen must show the result at once, read it from Payment directly.
A prototype microservice system has a browser calling services by fixed address, one database per service shared by its instances, and a single message broker. It must now scale each service to several instances. Explain what to add and which failure points to address.
Add a service registry and server-side discovery: clients call a router, here the API gateway, at a well-known location, and it looks up available instances and load-balances across them. The gateway also gives each client a suitable API, aggregates calls and translates protocols. Scaling instances leaves the shared databases and the message broker as single points of failure, so both need high availability, and the gateway and registry must themselves be replicated.
