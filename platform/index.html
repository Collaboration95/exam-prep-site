Whole-course questions

Try after all the days. Explain your choices before opening the answer.

Maid2Order expects to grow from 5 to 50 maid agencies and add a corporate-customer interaction within a year, while releasing weekly without downtime. Explain how you would fit together its domain boundaries, APIs, data stores and release process, and name the riskiest trade-off.
Keep the bounded contexts (customer, agency, maid order, maid loan, billing, payment) as separately owned services with their own schemas, so the new interaction plugs in as new entities or contexts rather than changes to the core. Expose them through level 2 REST APIs with major-only versioning behind a gateway, and integrate between contexts with domain events, plus sagas with compensation where a flow spans services. Choose each store by workload, relational for bills and payments for example. Release with rolling or canary deployments that stay backward compatible with the data, and watch traces, yield and task success. The riskiest trade-off is eventual consistency between contexts: it buys independent releases and scaling, but users can briefly see in-progress or compensated states, so the screens and support processes must allow for that.
An internal developer platform team is asked to offer a 'create a new service' golden path. Using the whole course, say what the golden path should give every team by default, and what should remain each application team's decision.
By default: a service template built on the platform's framework, REST conventions (plural nouns, correct verbs and status codes, idempotency keys, major-only versioning) and gateway registration with central authentication and rate limits. Also service discovery by alias, circuit breakers and timeouts through interceptors, trace-ID propagation, centralised logging and standard metrics, and a canary or rolling deployment pipeline with rollback. Security checks should be built in as guardrails (shifting down), not handed to developers as a checklist. Each team should still own its bounded context and domain model, its choice of data model for its workload, and its event contracts. Standardising those as well would bring back the shared-model coupling that DDD exists to avoid.
